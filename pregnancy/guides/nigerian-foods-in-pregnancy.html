Are tiger nut, bitter kola, garden egg, cloves and Lipton safe in pregnancy?

Last updated: October 2026

Written by Cynthia Obinatu, community and editorial lead · Checked against published guidance from National Health Service, PubMed Central, Tropical Journal of Pharmaceutical Research, via PubMed Central, United Nations Children's Fund and World Health Organization. Not yet reviewed by a clinician, so read it with our medical policy.

Short answers first, because you are probably standing in a kitchen. Tiger nut is fine as a food. The tiger nut drink sold in a bottle at the roadside is a different question. Garden egg is a vegetable and is fine. Cloves in your jollof are fine, and concentrated clove oil is not the same thing. Lipton and every other tea counts towards a caffeine limit, and the bigger issue is drinking it with meals. Bitter kola is the one to leave alone, because nobody has tested it in pregnant women.

Where these answers come from. Where guidance exists we follow the National Health Service and the World Health Organization. Where it does not, we say so. This guide has not been reviewed by a clinician, and it does not replace your antenatal clinic.

Is tiger nut good for pregnancy?

Tiger nut is a small tuber that people call a nut. It is a food like any other, and there is no reason to avoid it in pregnancy. The fibre helps when constipation arrives in the second trimester and everybody pretends it has not.

What it does not do is the long list attached to it online. It will not make your baby fairer, it will not determine the sex, and it will not make labour easier. Nobody has run those studies, and the confidence of the person telling you is not evidence.

The caution is about kunun aya, the drink. A tiger nut drink is soaked, ground, strained and sold unrefrigerated, often made with untreated water and handled by several people. The National Health Service says to avoid unpasteurised drinks in pregnancy, because your resistance to food-borne infection drops while you are pregnant. If you want kunun aya, make it at home with clean water, keep it cold, and drink it the same day.

Is bitter kola safe in pregnancy?

We would leave it. It has not been shown to be dangerous. It has not been shown to help either, so nothing supports eating it in pregnancy.

Bitter kola is a stimulant. That is why people chew it, and why traders chew it through a long day. One bite of a food with a stimulant in it is different from taking a stimulant on purpose, every day, for an effect. Bitter kola is usually used the second way. No trial has measured what a regular bitter kola habit does to a pregnancy. Anyone telling you it is proven safe, or proven to prevent anything, is telling you something they cannot know.

If you have already been chewing it, do not panic. Stop, and tell your midwife or doctor at your next antenatal visit.

Does garden egg have side effects in pregnancy?

Garden egg is a vegetable. Eat it. The searches about first-trimester and third-trimester side effects look for a danger the evidence does not describe. The answer is the same in every trimester.

Raw vegetables carry whatever was on them when they were picked and handled. Wash garden eggs properly in clean water, as you would any raw produce. That is a hygiene point, and it applies to every vegetable.

Can cloves cause a miscarriage?

There is no good evidence that cloves used as a spice in food cause miscarriage. The amount of clove in a pot of jollof is tiny, people have cooked with it for centuries, and no study connects normal cooking use to pregnancy loss.

Concentrated clove preparations are a separate question. Clove oil, clove water drunk daily as a remedy, or a strong extract taken deliberately are not the same as a spice, and they have not been tested in pregnancy. The general principle holds for every plant on this page. A pinch in food is food, and a concentrated dose taken for an effect is closer to a medicine. Talk to a clinician about any medicine.

Many women ask this because they are afraid of the answer. Most early miscarriages happen because of a chromosomal problem in the pregnancy itself. They do not happen because of something the mother ate, drank or did. If you have lost a pregnancy, it was almost certainly not the cloves.

Is Lipton good for a pregnant woman?

Tea is allowed. Two points matter.

The first is caffeine. The National Health Service advice is no more than 200mg of caffeine a day in pregnancy, and it names tea alongside coffee, cola, energy drinks and chocolate. Tea sits well below coffee per cup, so a few cups a day is not the problem. Counting only the coffee, and forgetting the tea, the Coke and the chocolate, is the problem.

The second matters more here and is rarely mentioned. Tea drunk with a meal reduces how much iron your body takes from that meal, and anaemia in pregnancy is common across Nigeria. If you are taking iron tablets or eating for iron, the fix costs nothing. Move the tea an hour away from the meal. You do not have to give it up.

Herbal teas are not automatically safer for being herbal. The National Health Service specifically says to avoid herbal teas containing liquorice root in pregnancy.

How to judge the next thing someone tells you

None of this is unusual, and you are not being gullible. In a study of 595 pregnant women across three Nigerian zones, more than two thirds said they had used herbal medicines. The pressure is real. It usually comes wrapped in love, so it is hard to refuse out loud.

You will be handed more of these, usually by someone who loves you. These questions sort most of them quickly.

  • Is this food or a dose? Eating tiger nut is one question. Drinking agbo every day for an effect is another, and that one needs a clinician.
  • Is it raw, unpasteurised or sold unrefrigerated? That is a known risk, and it applies to any drink.
  • Is anyone promising a result, such as an easier labour or a baby of a certain sex? A promise like that needs evidence, and for almost all of these there is none.

Old knowledge still counts. The specific promises attached to it online were added later, by people selling something or repeating what they read.

When to see a doctor

Talk to your doctor or midwife if you have been taking any plant, seed or bark daily as a remedy, if you are on iron tablets and unsure how to take them, or if you are bleeding, cramping or worried about the pregnancy for any reason. Take the actual thing, or a photograph of it, to the appointment.

Common questions

Is tiger nut good for a pregnant woman?
Yes as a food. Tiger nuts are fine to eat and the fibre helps with constipation. The claims that it changes the baby's complexion, sex or your labour are not supported by evidence. Be careful with roadside tiger nut drink. It is unpasteurised.
Is kunun aya safe in pregnancy?
Not when it is bought at the roadside. Roadside kunun aya is unpasteurised, often made with untreated water and kept unrefrigerated. Your resistance to food-borne infection is lower in pregnancy. Made at home with clean water, kept cold and drunk the same day, it is fine.
Is bitter kola safe in pregnancy?
We would avoid it. It is a stimulant and no study has tested regular use in pregnant women, so nobody can call it safe or beneficial. If you have been taking it, stop and mention it at your next antenatal visit.
Does garden egg have side effects in pregnancy?
No. It is a vegetable and there is no evidence of harm in any trimester. Wash it properly in clean water, as with any raw produce.
Can cloves cause a miscarriage in early pregnancy?
There is no evidence that cloves used as a spice in cooking cause miscarriage. Concentrated clove oil or clove water taken daily as a remedy is a different matter and has not been tested in pregnancy. Most early miscarriages are caused by a chromosomal problem. Something you ate did not cause it.
Can a pregnant woman drink Lipton tea?
Yes, within the caffeine limit of 200mg a day. The limit counts tea, coffee, cola, energy drinks and chocolate together. Drink it away from meals, because tea reduces iron absorption and anaemia in pregnancy is common.
Which herbal teas should be avoided in pregnancy?
The National Health Service names herbal teas containing liquorice root as ones to avoid. Herbal does not mean tested. Check anything you drink daily.
