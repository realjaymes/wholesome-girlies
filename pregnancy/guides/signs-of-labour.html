Signs of labour: how to know delivery is close and when to go in

Last updated: November 2026

Written by Cynthia Obinatu, community and editorial lead, and James Praise, founder and researcher · Checked against published guidance from National Health Service, PubMed Central and World Health Organization. Not yet reviewed by a clinician, so read it with our medical policy.

The main signs of labour are contractions that come regularly and get stronger, a show (sticky, jelly-like pink mucus from the vagina), your waters breaking, backache, and an urge to use the toilet as the baby's head presses down. The National Health Service (NHS) says to call your midwife or hospital when contractions come every 5 minutes or more often. Call straight away, day or night, if your waters break, you bleed, the baby is moving less than usual, or you are under 37 weeks.

Early labour can last hours or even days, so you do not need to rush in at the first tightening. What helps is a plan made before then: transport arranged, your bag packed and the hospital's number saved on your phone.

What this covers. This guide is for women who are pregnant now and want to know what labour feels like at the start and when to go to the hospital. It does not replace your midwife or doctor. This guide has not been reviewed by a clinician.

How do I know my body is getting ready for delivery?

Your body gives several signs, and you may not get all of them. The NHS lists these:

  • Contractions or tightenings. In labour they come more often and get stronger.
  • A show. The NHS describes it as sticky, jelly-like pink mucus. It is pink because it carries a little blood.
  • Your waters breaking. This can come as a gush or a trickle.
  • Backache. Some women get a heavy, aching feeling.
  • An urge to go to the toilet, caused by the baby's head pressing on your bowel.

You can call your hospital whenever you think labour may have started. The next sections cover when to go in.

How do I know if delivery is close, or if it is just Braxton Hicks?

Many women feel tightenings for weeks before labour. The NHS calls these Braxton Hicks contractions. They may feel uncomfortable but are usually painless, they do not last long, they do not come often, and they do not build up.

Labour contractions become stronger and more regular. Time them on your phone from the start of one to the start of the next. If they come every 5 minutes or more often, the NHS says to call your midwife or hospital.

How do you feel 2 days before labour?

No sign tells you the exact day. Some women notice backache, a show or more tightenings in the days before, and some notice nothing until contractions start. The NHS says the early stage of labour can take hours or sometimes days, and that you will probably be advised to stay at home during it.

While you wait at home, the NHS suggests moving around, drinking fluids, having a snack if you are hungry, slow breathing, and a back rub from whoever is with you. It says paracetamol is safe to take and a warm bath may help.

How do I know if my cervix is open for delivery?

A midwife or doctor examines you to see how far your cervix has opened. The NHS explains that in early labour the cervix (the neck of the womb) softens and starts to open. Active labour, which the NHS calls established labour, starts when it has opened to about 4 centimetres and contractions are stronger and more regular. In a first pregnancy, the time from there to fully open is usually 8 to 18 hours, and about 5 to 12 hours for a second or third baby.

If you go in early and the midwife finds the cervix has only just started to open, the early stage is still going on. It is not a mistake to go.

When should I go to the hospital?

Call your midwife or labour ward for advice when contractions are regular and coming every 5 minutes or more often, or whenever you think you are in labour, says the NHS. If your hospital told you something different at antenatal clinic, follow what they said.

Do not wait if your waters break. The NHS says it is usual for labour to start within 24 hours of the waters breaking, and that you should tell your midwife immediately if the waters are smelly or coloured. Wear a pad. Do not use a tampon.

Some women in Nigeria reach hospital late after their waters break. A study at the University of Benin Teaching Hospital looked at 110 women whose waters broke at term before labour started. Only 65.5% reached the hospital within 24 hours, and 34.5% came after. Arriving late was linked to lower social class.

Labour can also start suddenly. In a study of women in Enugu, Kwara and Kano states, labour coming on suddenly was the reason women most often gave for giving birth at home when they had planned to give birth in a hospital or health centre. One woman in Kano said she could not make it in time.

Is it safe to give birth at 37 weeks and 3 days?

A baby born from 37 weeks is no longer premature. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine define 37 weeks 0 days to 38 weeks 6 days as early term, and 39 weeks 0 days to 40 weeks 6 days as full term, in their joint definition of term pregnancy. So a baby born at 37 weeks and 3 days is early term. If your doctor has a reason to watch your pregnancy closely, ask what it means for you.

Before 37 weeks is different. The NHS says to contact your midwife or maternity unit straight away if you have regular contractions or tightenings, period-type pains, a gush or trickle of fluid, or unusual backache before 37 weeks.

How can I go into labour at 38 weeks naturally?

There is no proven way. The NHS says there are no proven ways of starting labour yourself at home. It says having sex will not cause harm, but you should avoid it once your waters have broken.

Be careful with anything an aunty or a herbal seller gives you to drink to start labour. Ask your doctor or midwife before you take anything.

If your pregnancy goes past your due date, the hospital has its own steps. The NHS says a membrane sweep, an internal examination where the midwife or doctor sweeps a finger around the cervix during an internal examination, is usually offered after 39 weeks. Some women find it uncomfortable, and there can be cramping and some bleeding afterwards. Induction is offered if labour has not started by 41 weeks.

Who can be with me in labour?

The World Health Organization (WHO) says women have the right to a companion of their choice throughout labour and birth, and the freedom to move around in the early stages of labour. Ask at your antenatal clinic who can stay with you, and when.

Plan the journey early. In a study of 340 pregnant women in Ogun State, 89.1% had arranged transport and 83.2% had set money aside for the birth. Save the hospital number, agree who drives, and keep money for the car or taxi where you can reach it. Our hospital bag checklist and birth plan builder help you get ready from about 36 weeks.

When to see a doctor

Call your midwife, labour ward or doctor straight away, even in the middle of the night, if any of these happen. The NHS lists them as reasons not to wait until the next day:

  • your waters break
  • you have bleeding from the vagina
  • your baby is moving less than usual
  • you are under 37 weeks and think labour may be starting
  • a contraction lasts more than 2 minutes
  • you have 6 or more contractions in 10 minutes

Also call if the waters are smelly or coloured. If you cannot reach the hospital by phone, go in.

Common questions

What are the first signs of labour?
The first signs are contractions that come regularly and get stronger, a show, or your waters breaking. Some women also get backache or an urge to use the toilet.
How do I tell if labour is close at 38 weeks?
At 38 weeks, watch for contractions that come regularly and get stronger, a show, or your waters breaking. Braxton Hicks tightenings do not come often and do not build up.
Should I go to the hospital when my waters break?
Call your midwife or hospital straight away. Labour usually starts within 24 hours of the waters breaking, and coloured or smelly waters need to be checked at once.
How long does labour last for a first baby?
The early part can take hours or days. Once established labour starts, the NHS says the time to fully open is usually 8 to 18 hours for a first baby.
