Birth Plan Builder

How the birth plan builder works

Answer the questions that apply to you and skip the rest. Choose who you want with you, how you feel about pain relief, the kind of room you would like, whether you want to move around in labour, and how you plan to feed the baby. Add anything else the team should know in the last box, such as a fear of needles, a past difficult birth or the language you are most comfortable in. Press Create my birth plan and it turns your answers into one short page. Print it or save it as a PDF, and keep a copy in your hospital bag. Your answers are saved only on this phone, and nothing is sent to us.

What your birth plan means on the day

A birth plan tells your midwife, nurses and doctors what you want. The National Health Service (NHS) describes it as a way of letting them know what you want to happen during labour. The same page says birth does not always go to plan and things may have to change at the last minute. So read your plan as a list of wishes. It is a starting point for a conversation, and it guarantees nothing. If the team needs to do something different to keep you or the baby safe, ask them to explain why.

The NHS also says to talk your choices over with your midwife so they understand them and can advise you. Take the printed plan to an antenatal visit and go through it with your midwife or doctor. Ask which of your wishes the hospital can meet.

What should I put in a birth plan?

The NHS checklist covers where you want to give birth, who you want with you, whether you want to stay active and which positions you might use, pain relief, skin-to-skin contact (the baby placed on your bare chest) straight after birth, how you plan to feed, and whether you agree to the baby having vitamin K if needed. The builder covers several of the choices on that checklist. Use the last box for the rest.

Can my husband or mother be with me in labour?

The World Health Organization (WHO) says women have the right to a companion of their choice throughout labour and birth. In a study of 340 pregnant women in Ogun State, 88.2% had already chosen who would go with them to the hospital, and 58.8% named their husband. Hospital rules differ, so ask at your antenatal clinic who may stay with you, at which stage, and whether that changes for a caesarean section.

What pain relief can I ask for?

The NHS lists gas and air, an injection of pethidine, an epidural, a TENS machine (small pads on your back that give a tingling feeling) and water. Not every Nigerian hospital offers all of these. Ask your hospital which options it has, whether they are available at night, and what they cost. "Not sure yet, guide me on the day" is a fine answer.

Can I move around and eat during labour?

The WHO says women should have the freedom to move around during the early stages of labour and choose their position for birth, and it includes food and drink in labour among its recommendations. Hospitals have their own rules, especially if you might need a caesarean section, so ask your midwife what applies to you.

Why plan for feeding before the baby comes?

The WHO recommends skin-to-skin contact between mother and baby straight after birth and starting breastfeeding within the first hour. Writing it in your plan tells the team you want that time with your baby. If you are not sure about breastfeeding yet, "I would like to try, with support" asks for help without promising anything.

Why bring a plan at all?

Women in a study in Benue State described being shouted at and treated with disrespect in maternity care. A written plan gives your companion and the hospital team a clear record of your wishes, even when the staff change shift. When you feel labour starting, our guide to the signs of labour covers when to call the hospital, and the hospital bag checklist covers what to bring.

Common questions

Do I need a birth plan?
No, but it helps. The NHS describes it as a way to let your midwife and doctors know what you want, and to talk your choices over with them before labour.
What if my birth does not go to plan?
Your plan may need to change during labour. The NHS says things may have to change at the last minute. Your team will put your safety and your baby's first.
Who can be with me during labour?
The WHO says women have a right to a companion of their choice. Hospital rules differ in Nigeria, so ask your antenatal clinic who can stay with you.
When should I write my birth plan?
Write it before your due date and take it to an antenatal visit to go through with your midwife or doctor.
Is my birth plan saved anywhere?
Only on this phone. Nothing is sent to us. Print it or save it as a PDF to share it.
