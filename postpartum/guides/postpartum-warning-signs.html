Postpartum warning signs: when to get help now

Last updated: September 2026

Written by Cynthia Obinatu, community and editorial lead · Checked against published guidance from BMJ Open, via PubMed Central, National Health Service and World Health Organization. Not yet reviewed by a clinician, so read it with our medical policy.

Most of what happens after birth is uncomfortable and normal. A few things are not, and the difference is worth knowing before you need it. This page gives you countable thresholds rather than the word "excessive", because "excessive" is exactly the word that makes a woman wait.

If you are reading this at 3am and worried. Go. Do not finish the page first. Nobody at a clinic will mind that you came and it turned out to be nothing, and a great deal of what follows is treatable when it is caught early. This guide helps you recognise a sign. It is not a diagnosis, and it has not been reviewed by a clinician.

Why "heavy bleeding" needs a number

Severe bleeding after childbirth is the single largest cause of maternal death worldwide. The World Health Organization puts severe bleeding, infection, high blood pressure, delivery complications and unsafe abortion together at around 75 per cent of all maternal deaths, and says most of them are preventable, because the treatments are well known. What stands between a woman and that treatment is usually time.

Researchers in Yola asked women how they knew their own bleeding had gone too far. The answers describe a threshold that arrives far too late.

Fainting is not the test. It is the consequence. Use a pad instead, because a pad is countable and a feeling is not.

For you: get help today if you have

  • Bleeding that soaks a maternity pad in an hour, or that is getting heavier rather than lighter, or clots bigger than a 50 naira coin. The National Health Service says to tell your midwife if you are losing blood in large clots.
  • A fever, shivering, or feeling generally unwell. Infection after childbirth is one of the big causes of maternal death and one of the most treatable when caught early.
  • Pain that is getting worse rather than better, particularly in your abdomen, or pain around stitches with redness, heat or discharge.
  • A bad headache, blurred or flashing vision, or sudden swelling in your face and hands. Blood pressure problems do not end at delivery and can start after it.
  • Chest pain, breathlessness, or a hot, painful, swollen area in one calf. These are clot signs and they are an emergency, not a wait-and-see.
  • Vaginal discharge that smells foul. Lochia has a smell. It should not be offensive.
  • A red, hot, painful wedge on one breast with flu-like aching. That is mastitis, and it needs treating rather than pushing through.
  • Being unable to pass urine, or leaking that is not improving.

One more, because it is the one women most often talk themselves out of. If something feels badly wrong and you cannot name it, that is a reason to go. You have carried this body for longer than any clinician has known it.

When to see a doctor

If you have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, or you feel you cannot cope, tell someone you trust today and contact a health professional. Postnatal depression is common and it is treatable, and the National Health Service describes it as an illness rather than a failing. It is not the baby blues, which lift within about two weeks. It does not lift, and it responds to help.

For your baby: get help today if they

  • Are feeding much less than usual, or are hard to wake for feeds. A newborn who will not wake to eat is a newborn to take in.
  • Have a fever, or feel unusually cold. In a small baby, too cold matters as much as too hot.
  • Have far fewer wet or dirty nappies than normal. Nappies are the most reliable thing you can count.
  • Are breathing fast, grunting with each breath, or pulling in under the ribs.
  • Are becoming more yellow in the skin or the whites of the eyes, especially in the first week, or look very pale or mottled.
  • Are floppy, unusually still, or crying in a way that is not like them.
  • Have a rash that does not fade when you press a glass against it.

Several of those overlap with sepsis, which moves quickly in babies. The National Health Service keeps a plain list of sepsis symptoms worth reading once while nothing is wrong.

The six weeks are not the whole window

The six-week check is a milestone, not a finish line. Blood pressure problems, clots, infection and postnatal depression can all begin after it, and a woman who has been discharged often assumes she has been signed off. You have not been. If a new sign appears in month two or month four, it counts exactly as much as one in week one.

How to be taken seriously when you go

Bring numbers. Say how many pads in how many hours, rather than "a lot". Say what your temperature was if you took it. Say when it started and whether it is worsening. A clinician can act on a number immediately and has to work to get there from "I do not feel right".

Take someone with you if you can, especially if you are exhausted. And if you are sent home and the thing gets worse, go back. Going back is not a complaint about the first visit. It is new information.

Common questions

How much bleeding after birth is too much?
Soaking a maternity pad within an hour, bleeding that gets heavier instead of lighter, or clots larger than a 50 naira coin all mean get help now. Count pads rather than judging by feel, because the feeling-based thresholds women use, such as feeling faint on standing, arrive far too late.
How long does bleeding after birth last?
A few weeks. It is heaviest in the first days, then lightens and changes colour from red to brown to a pale discharge. Bleeding that becomes heavier again after lightening is worth a call.
What are the warning signs of infection after birth?
A fever or shivering, pain that worsens instead of easing, foul-smelling discharge, or redness, heat and discharge around stitches. Infection after childbirth is a major cause of maternal death and is very treatable when caught early.
Is it normal to feel low after having a baby?
A short dip is. The baby blues affect many women and lift within about two weeks. A low mood that deepens, or lasts beyond that, or brings thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, is postnatal depression. It is common, it is an illness rather than a failing, and it is treatable.
When should I take my newborn to hospital?
If they are feeding much less or are hard to wake, have a fever or feel unusually cold, have far fewer wet nappies, are breathing fast or grunting, are becoming more yellow or look very pale, are floppy or unusually still, or have a rash that does not fade under a pressed glass.
Can something go wrong after the six-week check?
Yes. Blood pressure problems, clots, infection and postnatal depression can all begin after six weeks. Being discharged is not being signed off. A new sign in month two counts as much as one in week one.
What if I go and it turns out to be nothing?
Then it was nothing, and you found out. No clinic minds. The cost of a wasted trip is an afternoon. The cost of waiting on the wrong one is not comparable.
