Omugwo: what it means, what happens, and how to plan it

Last updated: October 2026

Written by Cynthia Obinatu, community and editorial lead

Omugwo is the Igbo tradition of caring for a woman after she gives birth. Her mother, or another senior woman in the family, moves in for weeks. She cooks, bathes the new mother, takes the baby at night and teaches her how to feed and care for a newborn. The new mother rests and recovers while someone else runs the house. It usually lasts one to three months, and Nigerian families practise it across Nigeria and abroad, under different names.

What this guide is. It explains the tradition and how families plan it today. It is educational, not medical advice. The health points link to their sources, and the guide has not yet been reviewed by a clinician. For what your body is doing after birth, read our recovery timeline, and for signs that need a doctor, read postpartum warning signs.

What is the meaning of omugwo?

Omugwo means the care and nursing given to a woman who has just given birth. It is an Igbo word (Ọmụgwọ), and it names both the weeks of care and the visit that makes them possible. When a family says the grandmother is coming for omugwo, they mean she is coming to run the household so the new mother can recover.

It runs for weeks, and the senior woman has a job to do the whole time.

What omugwo is called in English

There is no single English word for it, and any page that gives you one is stretching. The closest English descriptions are postpartum confinement care or the older British term lying-in. Both describe a period of rest after birth. Neither says that a specific woman, usually your own mother, comes and does the work.

In practice, Nigerian families abroad usually just say omugwo, or explain it as "my mum is coming to help with the baby".

What do you call a mother after giving birth?

In English she is a new mother, and in clinical writing a postpartum or postnatal woman. Omugwo is the care she receives, and the senior woman who comes to give it is said to be doing omugwo for her.

What actually happens during omugwo

The details vary by family, but most families follow the same pattern. The senior woman arrives at or shortly after the birth and takes over the running of the home.

  • She cooks. Specific foods are prepared for recovery and for milk supply, commonly pepper soup, ọfọ nsala and other hot, peppery broths.
  • She handles the baby at night so the mother can sleep between feeds.
  • She bathes the mother, traditionally with hot water, and massages the abdomen. Practices around heat and massage vary a great deal between families, and we set out what to ask a doctor first further down.
  • She teaches. She shows you how to latch the baby, bathe a newborn and tie the baby on your back, and what a normal cry sounds like.
  • She takes visitors off you. In a Nigerian household that is a big job.

The new mother does as little as possible except feed her baby and heal.

How long is omugwo after birth, and how many months in Igbo land?

Omugwo usually lasts one to three months in Igbo families. A research paper on Igbo grandmothers in Southeast Nigeria puts the range at seven weeks to six months, and a nurse researcher writing on her own family's omugwo gives the usual range as thirty to ninety days. Her own mother stayed four months. One to two months is typical for a mother going back to work.

There is no fixed rule. It lasts as long as the family can afford, in time and in money, and the first six weeks are the ones that matter most.

Who does omugwo in Igbo land?

The new mother's own mother does it by default. That is the expectation in most Igbo families, and it is deliberate: a woman is cared for by the person she can be at her worst in front of.

When she cannot come, the role passes to the mother-in-law, an aunt, an elder sister, or a senior woman in the church or compound. She may have died, be unwell, live too far away or be caring for someone else. Some families split it, with the mother's mother taking the first month and the mother-in-law the second. Increasingly, families in Lagos, Abuja and abroad pay for it. There are now businesses offering trained omugwo carers where no relative is available.

Who does omugwo in Yoruba land?

A senior woman in the family does the same work. Each family settles which woman it is, the new mother's mother or her mother-in-law. We have found no credible published source that sets a Yoruba rule. We also have not found a verified Yoruba word for omugwo, so we do not name one. This is one of the most searched questions about the practice, and a confident answer without a source would be a guess.

Other Nigerian groups have similar postpartum practices, with differences in the number of days, the food, the celebrations and the ceremonies, as the Hektoen International account notes. The word and the formality around it are Igbo. The idea is shared: a new mother should not be left alone to cook and clean while bleeding and learning to breastfeed. That idea is nearly universal on the continent and largely absent from the countries whose health websites dominate these search results.

How do you plan omugwo with your mother or mother-in-law?

Most arguments over omugwo are about logistics, and you can predict almost all of them.

  • Agree the dates early. Ask in the second trimester. If travel or a visa is involved, start earlier still.
  • Agree who sleeps where. A grandmother on a sofa for three months is a fight waiting to happen.
  • Agree the money. Say plainly who pays for the flight, the food and the time off. Unspoken assumptions here cause a lot of resentment.
  • Agree the boundaries. Settle feeding decisions, who is allowed to visit, and whether advice you disagree with gets followed. Our partner support planner is built for exactly this conversation.
  • Talk to your partner first. Omugwo puts a third adult in your home during the hardest weeks of your life. It works when both of you have agreed to it.

What if omugwo does not go well?

Omugwo is protective for most women, and it is also unpaid labour by an older woman who may be tired, unwell, or grieving her own losses. Research on Nigerian mothers has looked at both sides: the support is real, and so is the strain on the woman providing it and the friction when expectations clash.

It goes wrong when a grandmother takes over instead of supporting, when her advice contradicts what your midwife told you, or when your mother-in-law arrives instead of your mother and nobody discussed it. None of this means the tradition is wrong. It needs the same planning as anything else that involves three adults and no sleep.

If you dread the help instead of looking forward to it, say so to your partner before the baby arrives.

What to keep in omugwo

Much of omugwo is practical support for the first weeks. A woman who has just given birth needs rest, food, help and sleep, and a senior woman in the house can give her all four. The World Health Organization (WHO) calls the six weeks after birth a critical time for women, newborns, partners, parents, caregivers and families, and asks health systems to respect a family's cultural context (WHO, 2022). Keep these four things, and thank the woman who does them.

  • Rest. After a Caesarean, the National Health Service (NHS) in the United Kingdom says to expect to take things easy for several weeks, and to leave driving, exercise and lifting anything heavier than your baby until you feel able. That can take six weeks or so (NHS). The advice is written for Caesarean recovery. After any birth, ask your doctor or midwife what rest and movement suit your recovery.
  • Help with the house. Cooking, washing and handling visitors is the work omugwo is built around. A 2023 interview study of grandmothers in Southeast Nigeria describes them taking over household work so the mother can rest, and some participants said this support reduced stress for the new mother. It also records what the help costs the grandmother in tiredness, travel money and lost income (Innovation in Aging). Before omugwo begins, agree who pays for what, and make sure the woman giving the care has time to rest too.
  • Cooked food. The NHS says you do not need a special diet while breastfeeding, and recommends a varied diet and plenty of fluids (NHS). Omugwo can make this easier, with regular meals, a variety of foods and a drink within reach for feeds.
  • Someone to hold the baby so she can sleep. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) lists sleep and fatigue among the things a doctor should ask about at the full postpartum visit (ACOG Committee Opinion 736). A relative who looks after the baby while the mother sleeps gives her that rest.

What to check with a doctor or midwife first

These practices are common, and mothers and mothers-in-law usually offer them with care. Published guidance and Nigerian research raise questions about how some of them are done and whether they suit every mother or baby. Ask your doctor or midwife about them before you begin. If the family sees it differently, invite your mother or mother-in-law to join that conversation with the doctor or midwife. Our Omugwo: Your Mum or His Mum? quiz is a light way to open that conversation at home.

Hot water can burn, so ask first

Hot water bathing and hot compresses on the belly are common after birth. In a survey of 290 mothers at the University College Hospital in Ibadan, 51.7% had used a hot abdominal compress, and mothers and mothers-in-law helped in 46.2% and 36.6% of cases. The authors name hot water burns among the possible harms and ask for more research (Journal of Community Medicine and Primary Health Care). A 1989 hospital report from Zaria found that 31 of 35 women treated for burns after birth had taken ritual hot baths (International Journal of Gynecology and Obstetrics).

The WHO guideline lists warm or cold compresses as options for engorged breasts, based on what the mother prefers. That recommendation is about the breasts. Ask your doctor or midwife before using heat or pressure on the belly, especially near a Caesarean wound. The NHS asks mothers to contact a midwife or doctor if the wound becomes more red, painful or swollen (NHS).

Babies can be scalded too. A hospital in Jos treated 11 newborns for burns over eight years, and nine of the injuries came from a hot water bath. Most of those baths were given by the baby's mother or grandmother, and the authors recommend teaching safe bathing to caregivers in routine care before and after birth (Journal of the West African College of Surgeons, 2023). WHO recommends delaying a healthy full-term baby's first bath for at least 24 hours after birth (WHO, 2022).

Tying the belly with a wrapper or binder

Two reviews published in 2021 pooled trials of abdominal binders after Caesarean births. One review of six trials found lower pain and distress scores at 24 and 48 hours with a binder (International Journal of Gynecology and Obstetrics). The other, of four trials with 601 women, found less distress and no clear difference in pain (European Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology and Reproductive Biology). Both studied binders after Caesarean surgery, so they do not cover every wrap or every kind of birth. We did not find trials of binding after a vaginal birth. Ask your surgeon or midwife whether a binder suits you, how tight it should be and when to take it off.

Food and drink rules

The NHS says you do not need a special diet while breastfeeding, and advises a varied diet and plenty of fluids (NHS). The WHO guideline asks health workers to offer dietary advice to help prevent constipation after birth (WHO, 2022). Tell your midwife if a food rule leaves out a whole food group or leaves you unable to eat or drink enough.

Herbal mixtures

The NHS says not enough is known about herbal remedies to guarantee they are safe while breastfeeding, and asks mothers to check with a doctor, midwife or pharmacist (NHS). That statement is about missing evidence. It does not say any one mixture is harmful. Tell your doctor, midwife or pharmacist about any herbal mixture you plan to take while breastfeeding, and share its name or show the packet.

Anything put on the baby's cord needs a check first

WHO recommends clean, dry cord care. In places where many newborns die and babies are born at home, it recommends daily chlorhexidine on the stump for the first week (WHO, 2022). The NHS says to keep the stump clean and dry until it falls off, and to tell a midwife or doctor about any bleeding or discharge (NHS). Ask your midwife what, if anything, should go on your baby's cord stump. The right advice can depend on where the baby was born.

Nigerian research records other substances in use. A 2025 review of 11 Nigerian studies found methylated spirit, hot compresses and indigenous substances applied to the cord, and called for discouraging hazardous substances and promoting safer options (BMC Pediatrics). A 1992 report on 32 babies treated for infection around the cord at a hospital in Ilesa found hot fomentation and unsterile materials to be the apparent causes (Central African Journal of Medicine). A 2025 case report from The Gambia describes a 10 day old baby whose cord stump became infected after shea butter was applied, and who recovered after six days of antibiotics in hospital (Case Reports in Infectious Diseases). That is one baby. One case cannot show how often this happens or prove that shea butter was the cause.

Water and herbs for a young baby

WHO and the United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) advise feeding babies only breast milk for the first six months, and avoiding extra food or drink, even water, unless medically necessary (WHO and UNICEF). UNICEF adds that babies do not need water before six months, even in hot weather, because breast milk is mostly water. It also says water can be unclean and can take the place of milk feeds (UNICEF). Herbal drinks also count as extra drinks. If a breastfed baby under six months seems thirsty or unsettled, offer the breast and speak with a nurse or midwife. A survey of 365 mothers in Ihiala, Anambra State, found that 23.8% exclusively breastfed (BMC Pregnancy and Childbirth, 2025).

Staying indoors for the first weeks

Resting at home is part of omugwo. It can still leave room for the clinic visits recommended for mother and baby. WHO recommends postpartum care contacts in the first 24 hours, again between 48 and 72 hours, between 7 and 14 days, and in week six (WHO, 2022). ACOG recommends that every mother has contact with her care provider within three weeks and a full check no later than 12 weeks after birth (ACOG Committee Opinion 736). In an Ibadan survey of 678 mothers visited at home on days 8 to 10, 14.5% of the babies had had a check-up after birth (West African Journal of Medicine, 2022). Ask your clinic which visits and vaccines your baby needs, and book them before the family arrives.

When to see a doctor

Omugwo is care. It does not replace your postpartum checks at the clinic. Do not skip an appointment or wait out something that worries you because a family member is caring for you at home. Our postpartum warning signs guide sets out what needs a doctor the same day. Keep your clinic's number where everyone helping you can see it.

After a Caesarean, the NHS says to contact your midwife or doctor straight away if you have severe pain, leaking urine, pain when passing urine, heavy vaginal bleeding, a wound that becomes redder, more painful and swollen, pus or foul-smelling fluid from the wound, a cough, shortness of breath, or swelling or pain in your lower leg (NHS). Treat any burn or scald straight away, on you or on the baby, by holding it under cool running water for 20 minutes, then get medical advice. The NHS says to get advice for any child under 5 (NHS). Contact your midwife or doctor if the baby's cord stump bleeds or has any discharge.

This guide summarises the published sources and makes no promise about any practice. Your doctor or midwife can advise you on your recovery and your baby's care.

Common questions

What is the meaning of omugwo?
Omugwo is the Igbo word for the care and nursing given to a woman after she gives birth. Her mother or another senior woman in the family comes to stay, cooks for her, takes the baby at night and teaches her to care for a newborn so she can rest and recover.

Usually one to three months. Published accounts range from seven weeks to six months, and many families run it longer for a first baby. There is no fixed rule. It depends on what the family can afford in time and money. Families abroad are often limited by how long a visa allows.

Which omugwo practices are worth keeping?
Families can keep the practical care at the heart of omugwo: help with meals and the house, and someone to look after the baby while the mother rests. The National Health Service (NHS) says recovery after a Caesarean may mean taking things easy for several weeks. The World Health Organization (WHO) calls the six weeks after birth a critical time for women, newborns and their families.
Is hot water massage of the belly after birth safe?

A survey at University College Hospital, Ibadan, named burns as a possible harm of hot abdominal compresses but did not measure how often they happen. Can you put shea butter on a baby's cord?

The NHS says to keep the stump clean and dry until it falls off. A 2025 case report from The Gambia describes an infected cord stump in a baby of 10 days after shea butter was applied. One case cannot show how often this happens or prove that shea butter was the cause.
Should a young baby be given water or herbs?
WHO and the United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) advise feeding only breast milk for the first six months, with no extra food or drink, including water or herbal drinks, unless medically necessary. UNICEF says breast milk gives a baby the water needed, even in hot weather. If your baby seems unwell or unsettled, see a nurse, midwife or doctor.
Is an abdominal binder safe after birth or a Caesarean?
Two reviews published in 2021 looked at pain and distress after Caesarean births. Both found lower distress scores with a binder, and only one found lower pain scores. They did not show that every kind of wrap is safe after every kind of birth. Ask your surgeon or midwife whether a binder suits you, how tight it should be and when to take it off.
