Guide

Your postpartum recovery timeline, week by week

Last updated: September 2026

Written by Cynthia Obinatu, community and editorial lead · Checked against published guidance from American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, National Health Service and World Health Organization. Not yet reviewed by a clinician, so read it with our medical policy.

Everything before the birth is about the birth. Then the baby arrives, and your own body becomes the thing nobody scheduled an appointment for. This is what the weeks usually look like, what is normal in each of them, and where a caesarean recovery runs on a different clock.

Normal covers a wide range. Two women can have very different recoveries and both be fine. Use this to know roughly where you are, not to measure yourself against it. Anything on the warning signs list overrides every timeline on this page. This guide has not been reviewed by a clinician.

Week 1: the heaviest week

Bleeding, called lochia, is at its heaviest now and you will need maternity pads rather than ordinary ones. The National Health Service says to expect it to be quite heavy at first and to carry on for a few weeks. Afterpains, cramping as the womb shrinks back, are normal and often sharper while feeding.

Women describe the difference between normal and worrying bleeding in terms of feel rather than volume, which is understandable and is also why it gets missed.

That description is of bleeding that has already gone too far. Normal lochia soaks a pad over hours, not minutes. If you are reaching for the word "gushing", go in.

You are sore, you are not sleeping, and you are learning to feed a newborn at the same time. The most useful thing anyone can do this week is take something off you rather than hold the baby. If people are asking how to help, give them the kitchen and the laundry.

Weeks 2 to 3: the fog

Bleeding lightens and changes colour, from red to brown to a pale discharge. Many women meet a wave of tearfulness and overwhelm around now, the baby blues, which usually lifts within about two weeks.

If it does not lift, or it deepens, that is not you failing at this. Postnatal depression is common and treatable, and the National Health Service treats it as an illness rather than a character problem. The difference that matters is duration and direction: the blues lift, depression does not.

Weeks 4 to 5: some energy back

Most women start to feel a little more themselves. Short walks help. This is not the moment for hard exercise, because the core and the pelvic floor are still knitting, and pushing too early is how a problem that would have resolved becomes one that needs treating.

Week 6: the check that is about you

The six-week check is where somebody finally looks at how you are healing and how you are coping. Write your questions down before you go, because the appointment is short and the list evaporates in the room. Pain, sex, contraception, feeding, mood, all of it belongs there.

One thing to raise even if nobody brings it up. You can become pregnant again as soon as three weeks after birth, and the National Health Service is explicit that this holds even if you are breastfeeding and your periods have not returned. Contraception is a six-week-check conversation, not a later one.

And the six weeks are not a finish line. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists argues that postpartum care should be an ongoing process rather than a single encounter, with contact in the first three weeks and a full assessment no later than twelve. Blood pressure problems, clots, infection and postnatal depression can all start after week six. Being discharged is not being signed off.

If you had a caesarean, the clock is different

A caesarean is abdominal surgery, and recovery runs longer than a vaginal birth on almost every axis. The National Health Service guidance on recovering from a caesarean says to hold off on driving, exercise, carrying anything heavier than your baby, and sex, and that this may not be for six weeks or so. Start each one only when you can do it without discomfort, rather than on a date.

Watch the wound for redness, heat, swelling, or discharge, and take pain relief on a schedule rather than waiting until it hurts, because staying ahead of the pain is what lets you move, and moving is what prevents clots.

What to eat after a caesarean

There is no special healing food, and the searches assume there is. No fruit knits a wound faster, no drink speeds the scar, and anyone selling you one is selling you something. What genuinely matters after abdominal surgery is narrower and duller than that.

Fibre and fluid, from the first day. Constipation after abdominal surgery is very common, partly from the surgery and partly from the pain medication, and straining against a fresh incision is exactly as unpleasant as it sounds. Beans, pawpaw, oranges, vegetables, whole grains, and water through the day. If it has not moved after a few days, ask for a laxative rather than pushing.

Iron, because you lost blood. Women aged 19 to 49 need 14.8mg of iron a day, and a surgical delivery takes more blood than a vaginal one. Liver, beef, beans, dark green leaves such as ugu, and fortified cereals. Take any iron tablets away from tea, because tea reduces how much iron you absorb from the meal it sits with.

Enough protein and enough calories. Wounds are built from protein, and a woman eating too little while feeding a newborn heals slowly. Eggs, fish, chicken, beans, groundnut. This is not the moment for a weight-loss plan.

  • Can I eat bread after a caesarean? Yes. There is no reason to avoid it. Wholemeal gives you fibre that white bread does not.
  • Is milk good after a caesarean? It is fine, and it is useful protein. It is not a healing agent, and it does nothing for the scar. If it gives you gas, which is uncomfortable against a fresh incision, ease off it for a week.
  • Which fruit is best? The ones with fibre and water, because constipation is the actual enemy. Pawpaw, oranges, watermelon, banana. No single fruit does anything special.
  • What should I drink? Water, mostly, and more of it than feels necessary. Keep tea away from iron tablets and meals you are eating for iron. Nothing herbal taken daily as a remedy without asking your clinician first.

What nobody schedules an appointment for

Hair falls out in handfuls somewhere around month three or four, and it grows back. Your feet may have changed size permanently. Sex can hurt for months and that is worth raising rather than enduring. Leaking urine when you sneeze is common and it is treatable, so it is not something to accept as the price of having a child.

None of these are emergencies, which is precisely why they go unmentioned, and then each woman thinks she is the only one.

When to see a doctor

Bleeding that soaks a maternity pad in an hour, or gets heavier again after lightening. Fever or shivering. Pain that worsens rather than eases. A bad headache with vision changes. Foul-smelling discharge. Redness, heat or discharge at a caesarean wound. A hot, painful, swollen calf. Any thoughts of harming yourself or your baby. The full list is in the warning signs guide.

Common questions

How long does postpartum recovery take?
Most women feel physically recovered somewhere between six and eight weeks, and a caesarean usually takes longer. Emotional recovery runs on its own clock and often lags the physical one by months.
What to eat after a caesarean for fast recovery?
There is no food that speeds healing. What matters is fibre and fluid to prevent constipation, since straining against a fresh incision is painful, iron because you lost blood, and enough protein and calories to build tissue while feeding a newborn.
Can I eat bread after a C-section?
Yes. There is no reason to avoid it. Wholemeal is the better choice because it adds fibre, and constipation is the common problem after abdominal surgery.
Is drinking milk good after a C-section?
It is fine and it is useful protein. It does nothing specific for the wound or the scar. If it gives you gas, which is uncomfortable against a fresh incision, ease off for a week.
Which fruit is good after C-section delivery?
The ones carrying fibre and water, such as pawpaw, oranges, watermelon and banana. No single fruit speeds healing. The point is keeping things moving so you are not straining.
What are the best drinks after a C-section?
Water, and more than feels necessary. Keep tea away from iron tablets and iron-rich meals, because it reduces absorption. Avoid herbal preparations taken daily as remedies unless your clinician has agreed to them.
How long does bleeding last after birth?
A few weeks. It is heaviest in the first days, then lightens and changes from red to brown to a pale discharge. Bleeding that becomes heavier again after lightening needs a call.
How soon can I get pregnant after giving birth?
As soon as three weeks, and that holds even if you are breastfeeding and your periods have not come back. Raise contraception at the six-week check rather than assuming you have longer.
