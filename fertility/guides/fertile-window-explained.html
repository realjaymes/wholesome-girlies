Guide

Your fertile window explained

Last updated: September 2026

Written by Cynthia Obinatu, community and editorial lead · Checked against published guidance from American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, Journal of Medical Internet Research, via PubMed Central, National Health Service and World Health Organization. Not yet reviewed by a clinician, so read it with our medical policy.

You cannot conceive on most days of your cycle. There is a window of about six days, and everything else is waiting. Knowing where yours falls removes a surprising amount of the pressure, mostly because it tells you which days genuinely do not matter.

This is about finding a window, not guaranteeing one. Every method on this page estimates. None detects. If you are trying and the months are passing, a clinic will tell you more in one visit than a year of counting. This guide has not been reviewed by a clinician.

What the fertile window is

Ovulation is the release of an egg from an ovary. That egg survives about a day. Sperm survive far longer, up to about five days inside the body, which means sex several days before ovulation can still result in a pregnancy.

So the window is the five days before ovulation plus the day itself, roughly six days. The counterintuitive part is that most of the window sits before the event, not after it. Waiting until you are sure you have ovulated means you have largely missed it.

Why day 14 is wrong for most women

The day 14 rule comes from a 28-day cycle, and most cycles are not 28 days. The National Health Service puts the normal range at 21 to 35 days, which moves ovulation by a fortnight across that range.

Counting forward from your last period is the error. Ovulation is better estimated backwards, because the second half of the cycle is the steadier half. The National Health Service puts the time from egg release to the start of a period at around 10 to 16 days. So even the steady half has a six-day spread, which is why a calculator gives you a window rather than a date.

An analysis of 7,043 women's tracked cycles, published in the Journal of Medical Internet Research, found exactly this: most of the variation in cycle length lives in the first half, before ovulation, and assuming a mid-cycle release performs poorly. A method built on the backwards count outperformed the assumption that ovulation sits in the middle.

In practice: find your own average cycle length over three or four months with the period tracker, then let the ovulation calculator do the subtraction.

What your body tells you, and what it does not

Three signs are worth learning, and they differ in how much they are worth.

  • Cervical mucus. Clear, stretchy and slippery like raw egg white means the window is open now. This is the only common sign that points forwards, which makes it the most useful one for timing.
  • A small temperature rise. Your resting temperature lifts slightly after ovulation. It confirms that ovulation happened, which is useful for learning your pattern and useless for catching this month, because by the time it rises the egg is going or gone.
  • A one-sided twinge. Some women feel it, many do not, and it is not reliable enough to plan around on its own.

If you want certainty rather than estimation, ovulation predictor strips detect the hormone surge a day or two before release, and a clinic can scan and see it directly. Both tell you more than any calendar can.

Timing without turning it into a job

You do not have to hit one exact day, and trying to is how couples end up dreading the middle of the month. Sex every two or three days across the cycle covers the window without anyone having to check a chart first. It also removes the argument about whether tonight counts.

There is no evidence that saving up improves your odds, and long gaps can work against you. Regular is better than strategic.

A note on using this as contraception

Do not. Everything on this page estimates from a pattern, and a pattern cannot see the month your body behaves differently, which is exactly the month that matters. Illness, travel, stress and weight change all move ovulation. The same uncertainty that makes this a reasonable tool for trying makes it a poor one for avoiding.

It is worth holding onto while you count days. A window that is being timed perfectly still needs the other half of the equation to have been checked, and a semen analysis is quicker and cheaper than any of this.

When to see a doctor

If your cycles are very irregular, shorter than 21 days or longer than 35, or have stopped. If you have pain during sex or severe period pain. After twelve months of trying, or six months if you are over 35. After two or more miscarriages. Take both partners, and ask for a semen analysis at the first visit rather than the fifth.

Common questions

How many days a month can you get pregnant?
About six. The five days before ovulation plus the day of ovulation itself, because sperm survive up to about five days while the egg lasts about a day.
Is ovulation always on day 14?
No. Day 14 assumes a 28-day cycle, and the normal range is 21 to 35 days. Ovulation is better estimated by counting backwards from your next expected period, because the second half of the cycle is the steadier half, though even that runs about 10 to 16 days.
When is the best time to try to conceive?
In the two to three days before ovulation and on the day itself. Most of the fertile window sits before the release, not after, so waiting until you are certain you have ovulated means largely missing it.
What does fertile cervical mucus look like?
Clear, stretchy and slippery, like raw egg white. It is the most useful of the common signs because it points forwards, telling you the window is open now rather than confirming it has closed.
Does a temperature rise tell me when to try?
Not for this month. The rise happens after ovulation, so it confirms the pattern for next time rather than catching this one. It is worth tracking to learn your own cycle, not to time sex.
How often should we have sex when trying?
Every two or three days across the cycle. That covers the window without scheduling, and there is no evidence that saving up helps. Long gaps can work against you.
Can I use the fertile window as contraception?
No. It estimates from a pattern and cannot see the month your body does something different, which is the month it would matter. Illness, travel, stress and weight change all move ovulation.
