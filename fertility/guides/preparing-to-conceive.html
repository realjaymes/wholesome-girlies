Guide

Preparing your body to conceive, and what folic acid actually does

Last updated: September 2026

Written by Cynthia Obinatu, community and editorial lead · Checked against published guidance from American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, National Health Service and World Health Organization. Not yet reviewed by a clinician, so read it with our medical policy.

Most of conceiving is time, and very little of it is under your control. A short list of things genuinely helps, and the first one is worth starting three months before you try rather than on the day you decide.

What this covers. Preparation, not treatment. If you have been trying for twelve months, or six months if you are over 35, that is a clinic conversation rather than a preparation one. This guide has not been reviewed by a clinician.

Folic acid: 400 micrograms, starting before you try

The National Health Service recommends 400 micrograms of folic acid every day, ideally from three months before you conceive and through the first twelve weeks of pregnancy. Taking it reduces the chance of a neural tube defect, which is a problem with the brain or spine, such as spina bifida.

The timing is the whole point and it is the part people miss. A neural tube closes in the first month, often before a woman knows she is pregnant at all. By the time a test shows two lines, the window this supplement protects is largely past. That is why it belongs in the preparing phase rather than the pregnant phase.

You cannot reliably get the amount from food alone, which is why it is a supplement rather than a diet instruction. It is cheap and sold over the counter in pharmacies.

Who needs the higher 5mg dose

Some women are advised to take 5 milligrams rather than 400 micrograms, which is more than ten times the standard dose and needs a prescription. The National Health Service lists the situations: if you or the baby's father has a neural tube defect or a relative with one, if a previous pregnancy was affected, if you have diabetes, if you take medicine for epilepsy or HIV, or if you have a blood condition such as sickle cell anaemia or thalassaemia.

That last one deserves its own sentence here. Sickle cell is common enough in Nigeria that a meaningful number of women reading this fall into the higher-dose group and do not know it. If you have sickle cell disease, or you are unsure of your genotype, ask before you start rather than defaulting to the standard tablet.

Can folic acid boost fertility?

No. This is the most common thing people believe about it and it is not what the evidence supports. Folic acid protects the development of a pregnancy once it exists. It does not make conception more likely, it does not improve egg quality on demand, and taking more of it will not speed anything up.

That is not a reason to skip it. It is a reason to take it for the thing it actually does, and to keep looking elsewhere for the thing you were hoping it would do.

Does folic acid treat hormonal imbalance?

No. Folic acid is a B vitamin. It does not regulate hormones, it does not treat polycystic ovary syndrome, and it will not restore a cycle that has stopped or become erratic. Irregular or absent periods are worth investigating rather than supplementing, because the cause changes the treatment and a vitamin will not find it.

What are the benefits of folic acid when you are not pregnant?

Folate does real work in the body outside pregnancy. It is needed to make red blood cells, and a shortage causes folate deficiency anaemia, which the National Health Service describes as treatable and worth diagnosing early because some of its effects can become permanent if left.

The signs are unglamorous and easy to blame on ordinary life: tiredness, breathlessness, low energy, pale skin, mouth ulcers. None of them proves a deficiency, because all of them have a dozen other causes. A blood test does, and it is the only thing that does. If you are tired all the time, get tested rather than guessing which supplement to buy.

The rest of the list, in order of how much it matters

  • Stop smoking, and stop alcohol. Both affect fertility and both affect an early pregnancy you do not yet know about. This applies to him as well as to you.
  • Get to a steady weight rather than a target one. Being very underweight or very overweight can affect fertility. A crash diet while trying to conceive works against you on both counts.
  • Review every medicine and supplement with a pharmacist. Some need changing well before you try, not on the day you find out. This includes the herbal things, which are medicines that nobody wrote down.
  • Get the checks done while it is easy. A pre-pregnancy check matters most if you have a long-term condition. Dental work is far simpler now than later. Know your genotype and your blood group.
  • Have him tested too, early. A semen analysis is cheap and quick, and it is the most commonly skipped step. Testing only one person tells you about one person.

What is missing from that list is anything dramatic, and that is the honest shape of it. The preparation that works is boring, and the things sold as exciting have very little behind them.

When to see a doctor

Before you start trying if you have a long-term condition, take regular medication, have sickle cell disease, or have had a pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect. And after twelve months of trying, or six months if you are over 35, or sooner if your periods are very irregular or absent, or you have had two or more miscarriages. Take both partners.

Common questions

How much folic acid should I take before pregnancy?
400 micrograms a day, ideally starting three months before you conceive and continuing through the first twelve weeks of pregnancy. Some women need a 5mg prescription dose instead.
Can folic acid boost fertility?
No. Folic acid protects the development of a pregnancy once it exists. It does not make conception more likely and taking more does not speed anything up. Take it for what it does, which is preventing neural tube defects.
Does folic acid treat hormonal imbalance?
No. It is a B vitamin. It does not regulate hormones or treat polycystic ovary syndrome. Irregular or absent periods need investigating rather than supplementing, because the cause determines the treatment.
What are the benefits of folic acid when not pregnant?
Folate is needed to make red blood cells. Too little causes folate deficiency anaemia, which is treatable and worth catching early because some effects can become permanent if it is left.
What are the signs that you need folic acid?
Tiredness, breathlessness, low energy, pale skin and mouth ulcers can all point to it, and all have many other causes. Only a blood test tells you. If you are tired all the time, get tested rather than guessing at a supplement.
Who needs a 5mg dose of folic acid?
Anyone where you or the baby's father has a neural tube defect or a relative with one, a previous pregnancy was affected, or you have diabetes, a blood condition such as sickle cell anaemia or thalassaemia, or take medicine for epilepsy or HIV. It needs a prescription.
When should we see a doctor about not conceiving?
After twelve months of trying, or six months if you are over 35. Sooner if your periods are very irregular or absent, or you have had two or more miscarriages. Both partners should be seen, because testing only one tells you about only one.
