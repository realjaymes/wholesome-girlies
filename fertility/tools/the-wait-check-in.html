The Wait Check-in

Everybody asks when the baby is coming. Nobody asks how you are doing. Use this page to write down how the wait is sitting with you, privately. There is no score and it never tells you what is wrong with you. It points you to a real person when you need one.

Take these one at a time. There are no right answers.

  • Am I able to feel small moments of joy or peace?
  • Am I sleeping, or lying awake worrying about trying?
  • Do I still feel close to my partner, or has trying pushed us apart?
  • Can I be around pregnancy news without it breaking me?
  • Are the heavy feelings slowly easing, or getting heavier?

Whatever you wrote, please read this

There is no score here. If a few of these feel true for you, or one of them feels heavy, or the hard feelings have lasted, please reach out to someone. Asking for help takes strength.

Talk to your doctor or a counsellor. In Nigeria you can call the Mentally Aware Nigeria Initiative (MANI) on 0809 111 6264. If you are abroad, call Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK, or call or text 988 in the US or Canada. If you have known pregnancy loss, a counsellor or doctor can help with the grief, and you do not have to carry it alone. You matter.

Educational, not medical advice. This is a private place to reflect. It gives no diagnosis and no mental-health assessment, and it never replaces a doctor or a mental-health professional. What you write is saved only on your own device.

How the wait check-in works

There are six questions about how the wait is sitting with you this week: whether you still feel moments of joy, how you are sleeping, how close you feel to your partner, how you feel when someone announces a pregnancy, whether the heavy feelings are easing, and how you are doing overall. Take them one at a time and write as much or as little as you like. Clear wipes what you wrote. Everything stays on this phone, and nothing is sent to us. Come back whenever writing things down helps.

What your answers mean

There is no score, because the check-in is for you to notice how you are, and only you can judge that. If you read back what you wrote and a few answers feel heavy, or the heavy ones keep coming back month after month, take that as a sign to talk to someone. That can be your doctor, a counsellor, or the Mentally Aware Nigeria Initiative (MANI) on 0809 111 6264. Writing it down first often makes it easier to say out loud.

Why does trying to conceive feel so heavy?

Many women feel this weight. The World Health Organization (WHO) says about one in six people of reproductive age have infertility at some point in their lives. It says that couples, and women in particular, frequently face stigma, emotional stress, depression, anxiety and low self-esteem because of it.

Nigerian studies show the same weight. In a study of 415 women at Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital, 44.6% of women attending for infertility had signs of depression. A study of 341 women at the University of Benin Teaching Hospital found 42.5%. Most of it was mild, and it was common. Women who spoke to Zikoko in 2023 described the same thing. Ada, 29 (Zikoko changed the names of the women it spoke to), said: "There are days when you're happy and filled with hope. Other days, you just cry and cry." Fadeke, 33, said: "The pressure from our parents isn't helping. Every time they call, they end with prayers for a baby."

How long does it usually take to get pregnant?

Longer than the family thinks. The National Health Service (NHS) says more than 8 out of 10 couples where the woman is under 40 conceive naturally within a year of regular sex without contraception. So a few months of trying without a pregnancy is common. The NHS says to see a doctor after a year of trying, or sooner if you are 36 or older or already know of a fertility problem. The WHO defines infertility as no pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular unprotected sex.

When should I take a pregnancy test?

The days between ovulation and your period are often the hardest part of the month. The NHS says pregnancy tests are most reliable from the first day of a missed period, and that if you get a negative result but still think you might be pregnant, you can wait a few days and test again. Testing every morning before then can make the wait heavier. Our guide on the pregnancy test at home covers how to read the result.

Does stress stop you getting pregnant?

People will tell you to "just relax" as if worry were the cause. The Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority (HFEA), which regulates fertility clinics in the UK, says there is no conclusive evidence that stress affects the results of fertility treatment. Your feelings are not your fault, and looking after them matters for its own sake. The same page calls fertility treatment an emotional rollercoaster and says UK clinics must offer a counsellor.

Where can I get support in Nigeria?

Start with your doctor or the fertility clinic and ask whether they have a counsellor. The Mentally Aware Nigeria Initiative (MANI) runs a helpline on 0809 111 6264. If you are abroad, the numbers in the box above reach Samaritans in the UK and the 988 line in the US and Canada. If you have lost a pregnancy, our guide on chemical miscarriage explains what happens and when to see a doctor.

Common questions

Does the wait check-in give me a score?
No. There is no score and no diagnosis. It is a private place to write how the wait is sitting with you, and it points you to real people when you need them.
Is what I write private?
Yes. It is saved only on this phone and nothing is sent to us. Clear wipes it.
How long does it take most couples to get pregnant?
The NHS says more than 8 out of 10 couples where the woman is under 40 conceive within a year of trying.
When should I see a doctor about not getting pregnant?
The NHS says after a year of trying, or sooner if you are 36 or older or already know of a fertility problem.
Who can I call in Nigeria if I am struggling?
Your doctor, a counsellor, or the Mentally Aware Nigeria Initiative (MANI) on 0809 111 6264.
