Welcome

Nobody tells you how much the hoping costs.

The waiting. The counting of days. The quiet ache each time your period comes. The friends announcing, the aunties asking, the sense that everyone else finds this easy. If that is you, take a breath. You are not broken, you are not alone, and you have not done anything wrong.

This is one calm plan for the whole journey, instead of a pile of advice that all says something different. It helps you understand your body, do what you reasonably can, bring your partner in, know what your numbers mean, and hold steady through the waiting. What it will not do is promise you a baby or give you a false timeline. Real hope, never false hope.

How to use this

You do not have to start at the beginning. Open the part you need today. If understanding your cycle is the thing on your mind, start there. If it is the strain the waiting is putting on you, start with the mind and marriage part. The rest will wait.

One small thing at a time. Read a little, try one thing, come back later. Nothing here runs out.

Go at your own pace. There is no clock in here counting down. Trying can take time for many healthy couples, and taking time does not mean something is wrong.

Keep your doctor close. This helps you understand and ask better questions. It never replaces your doctor, and it never reads your test results for you. Anywhere something needs real care, we say so plainly.

Your safety first

Trying to conceive is not usually an emergency, but a few things are worth knowing.

See a doctor sooner, not later, if:

  • You are under 35 and have been trying for about a year without success.
  • You are 35 or older and have been trying for about six months.
  • Your periods are very irregular, very painful, very heavy, or have stopped.
  • You know you have a condition like PCOS, endometriosis, thyroid problems, or you have had a pelvic infection or surgery.
  • You or your partner have a known health issue that could affect fertility.

Seeing a doctor early is not giving up. It is giving yourself answers sooner.

Get help straight away if you have severe pain, especially on one side, or heavy bleeding, or you feel faint, particularly if there is any chance you could be pregnant. That needs urgent care.

For your mind: if the sadness or worry feels heavy, or it has lasted, please talk to someone. In Nigeria you can call the Mentally Aware Nigeria Initiative on 0809 111 6264. The waiting is hard, and you do not have to carry it alone.

Part 1 · Understand Your Body and Cycle

The more you understand your own cycle, the less of this feels like guessing. This part is the foundation everything else sits on, so give it a little time.

Your cycle, in plain words

Your menstrual cycle is the monthly rhythm your body runs on. Day 1 is the first day of your period. In the first half of the cycle, an egg is ripening in one of your ovaries. Around the middle, that ovary releases the egg. That release is called ovulation. If sperm meets the egg in the day or so after, conception can happen. If not, the womb lining sheds, your period comes, and the cycle starts again.

A cycle is often around 28 days, but anywhere from about 21 to 35 days is common, and yours may vary a little month to month. That is normal. The gap between ovulation and your next period is usually fairly steady (about two weeks), while the first half can vary more. This is why the day you ovulate is not always exactly "day 14," and why getting to know your own body beats following a fixed calendar rule.

Your fertile window

The fertile window is the small stretch of days each cycle when sex can lead to conception. Because sperm can live for a few days inside you, and the egg lives for only about a day after ovulation, the window is roughly the five days before ovulation plus the day of ovulation itself. Those are the days that matter most for timing.

You can estimate your likely window with the Ovulation and Fertile Window tool, and follow your cycle month to month with the Period Tracker. These give you an estimate to plan around and to ask better questions with, not an exact promise. Bodies are not calendars.

How to tell you have ovulated

Your body often gives signs around ovulation. None is perfect on its own, but together they help you learn your pattern.

  • Cervical mucus. This is the natural discharge you notice through the month. Around your fertile time it often becomes clearer, wetter, and stretchy, a bit like raw egg white. When you see that, your fertile days are likely near. This is one of the most useful, and cheapest, signs.
  • A small temperature rise. Your resting body temperature nudges up slightly after ovulation and stays up until your period. Taken first thing each morning with a basic thermometer and noted over a couple of cycles, it can confirm that ovulation happened, though it tells you afterwards, not before.
  • Ovulation predictor kits. These are simple sticks from the pharmacy that check a hormone called LH in your wee. LH surges just before ovulation, so a positive result means ovulation is likely coming within a day or so, which is a good sign to make time for each other.
  • Other signs. Some women feel a light one-sided twinge, a little more desire, or slight breast tenderness around this time.

Finding a tracking style that fits you

You do not need to do all of the above. Pick what fits your life. Some women just watch their mucus and log their periods; others add temperature or kits when they want more detail. The goal is gentle awareness, not a second job. If tracking starts to feel stressful, do less of it, not more.

Timing, without turning it into a chore

You do not need to have sex every single day, and you do not need a stopwatch. Regular sex, roughly every one to two days, across your fertile window covers it well. Some couples prefer to simply enjoy each other every couple of days through the whole month, so there is no pressure to perform on a schedule. Both approaches are fine, and the second one protects your relationship from the "sex on demand" strain we talk about in Part 5.

A few cycle truths worth knowing

  • A slightly different cycle length each month is usually normal.
  • You cannot conceive on just any day; the fertile window is short. This is often a relief to hear.
  • Spotting a day or two before your period is common.
  • When to check with a doctor: if your cycles are very irregular, very short or long, if you bleed between periods, or you never see any signs of ovulation. It does not mean something is wrong, but it is the kind of thing a doctor can look into, and worth doing sooner rather than later.

Part 2 · Do Everything You Reasonably Can

You cannot control everything about conceiving, and a great deal of it is not in anyone's hands. But there are sensible, evidence-based things that support your health and your body as you try. This is about doing what you reasonably can, calmly, not chasing miracles or blaming yourself for what you cannot control.

Start folic acid now

If you are trying, start taking folic acid (a B vitamin) every day, ideally before you conceive. It helps protect a developing baby's spine and brain in the earliest weeks, often before a woman even knows she is pregnant, which is why starting now matters. A daily prenatal vitamin that contains folic acid is the simplest way to cover it. Ask a pharmacist or your doctor about the right dose for you, especially if you have any health conditions, as some women need a higher dose.

Eat to nourish your body

You do not need a special or expensive "fertility diet." You need steady, balanced meals, and Nigerian everyday foods do this well.

  • Green leaves like ugu, spinach, and ewedu, for folate and iron.
  • Protein at meals: beans, eggs, fish, chicken, moi moi.
  • Whole grains and less of the heavily processed, very sugary foods where you can.
  • Fruit and vegetables across the day, for vitamins and antioxidants.
  • Iron-rich foods like liver, red meat, and beans, which matter for your cycle and your energy.
  • Enough water.

Aim for balance most of the time, not perfection. A calm, well-fed body is the goal, for both of you, because his diet matters too (see Part 3).

Look after the rest of your health

  • A healthy weight. Being well above or well below a healthy weight can affect your cycle and ovulation. Gentle, realistic steps matter far more than crash plans, which can do harm. Be kind to yourself here; this is support, not another stick to beat yourself with.
  • Sleep and stress. Rest as you can, and find small ways to lower stress: prayer, walks, time with people who lift you, less of what drains you. Lowering stress is good for you and your relationship. It is not a switch that turns conception on or off, so please never let anyone tell you that you are "just stressing too much."
  • Movement. Regular, moderate activity supports your health. Extreme, exhausting exercise every day is not necessary and can sometimes affect your cycle.
  • Caffeine, alcohol, and smoking. Keep caffeine modest (a cup or two a day). It is safest to stop smoking altogether and to limit or avoid alcohol while trying, for both of you. If you want help to stop smoking, your doctor can support you.

A word on supplements

Folic acid is the clear one to start. Some women are low in things like vitamin D or iron, but rather than buying a shelf of supplements on hope, ask your doctor what you actually need. A good prenatal vitamin plus real food covers most people.

A caution on "fertility" mixtures

Be careful with herbal mixtures, agbo, and "fertility teas" sold with big promises. Many are untested, some can be unsafe, especially taken alongside medicine, and none can guarantee anything. If you want to take anything herbal, ask your doctor or pharmacist first. Your money and your body are better spent on the steady, boring, proven things above.

Part 3 · It Takes Two

Here is something our world says far too quietly: conception is a couple's issue, and up to about half of the picture is on the man's side. It is never fair, and never accurate, to quietly assume it is only your problem. Yet so often it is the woman who carries all the worry, all the tests, and all the blame.

Bringing him in, gently

Many men do not know how much they matter here, and some feel awkward or defensive about being part of it. You do not have to turn it into a confrontation.

  • Share this part with him, or read it together over a quiet evening.
  • Frame it as a team thing: "This is something we do together, and there are simple steps that help us both."
  • Ask for one change at a time rather than handing him a long list when he is tired.
  • Notice and appreciate the steps he does take. People do more of what they feel seen for.

What supports sperm health

Sperm are made fresh over about two to three months, which is good news: changes he makes now can show up in a couple of months. The everyday things that help are simple.

  • Not smoking, and limiting alcohol.
  • A healthy weight and regular activity.
  • Keeping the area from getting too hot for long periods: very hot baths, saunas, a laptop resting on the lap, tight heat.
  • Reviewing any medicines, supplements, or "boosters" with a doctor, since some can affect sperm.
  • Eating well and managing stress, the same as for you.

What a semen analysis checks

If you see a doctor together, one of the first and simplest tests is a semen analysis. It looks at things like how many sperm there are (count), how well they move (motility), and their shape (morphology). It is a normal, routine test, done from a single sample, not a judgement of him as a man or as a husband. Understanding that it exists, and that it is quick and easy, takes a lot of the fear and shame out of asking for it.

We explain what the test is so you can ask for it and follow the conversation. Your doctor reads the result, not this guide.

The feelings on his side

Men can carry quiet fear and pride here too, and sometimes that comes out as avoidance or "let us just keep trying." Gentleness works better than pressure. Reminding him that getting checked is smart and normal, not an accusation, often helps him take the step.

Part 4 · Know Your Numbers and When to Get Help

Trying can come with tests and terms that nobody explains, and that turns an already hard time into a frightening one. This part puts the common ones in plain words, so you can walk into an appointment prepared instead of lost.

One rule sits over everything here. This part helps you understand, prepare, and ask the right questions. It never reads or interprets your own results, and it never replaces your doctor. Numbers only mean something in the hands of a professional who knows your full picture, so please do not try to diagnose yourself from a chart online.

Common tests, in plain terms

  • AMH (anti-Müllerian hormone). A blood test that gives a rough estimate of how many eggs you have left, sometimes called your egg reserve. It is one piece of the picture, not a yes-or-no on whether you will conceive. A number on its own, without a doctor, can frighten women for no good reason.
  • FSH and LH. Hormones involved in your cycle and in ovulation, usually checked with a blood test on a particular cycle day.
  • Progesterone. A blood test, often about a week after you think you ovulated, that can help show whether you ovulated that cycle.
  • Thyroid and prolactin. Sometimes checked too, because they can affect your cycle.
  • Semen analysis. The male test from Part 3 (count, movement, shape).
  • Scans. An ultrasound can look at your womb and ovaries; other scans can check whether your tubes are open.

You do not need to memorise these. You need to know they exist, so that when a doctor mentions one, you are not lost, and so you can ask what it is for and what happens next.

What a first fertility visit is often like

Knowing what to expect lowers the fear. A first visit usually means a conversation about your history and cycles, and often some of the tests above for both of you. It is a starting point, not a verdict. You are allowed to bring your partner, bring questions, and ask for more time.

Age and fertility, honestly and gently

Fertility does gradually decline with age, and the change becomes steeper from the mid-30s onward, for reasons of egg number and quality. We tell you this not to frighten you, but because honest information helps you make choices and decide when to seek help. It is a general pattern, not a verdict on any one woman. Plenty of women conceive later; the point is simply that time is one factor worth being aware of, and one reason not to wait too long before asking for help if things are not happening.

When it is time to see a specialist

Revisit "Your safety first" at the front. In short: about a year of trying under 35, about six months at 35 and over, or sooner if your cycles are irregular or you have a known condition. Seeing a doctor is not admitting defeat, and it is not "giving up on it happening naturally." It is getting answers and options while you have the most of them.

Part 5 · The Mind and the Marriage While You Wait

This is the part no one prepares you for, and for many women it is the hardest of all. Everything here is support, never a diagnosis. Where your mind needs real care, we will always point you to it.

What the waiting does

  • The fear. Every cycle carries hope and dread at the same time. It is exhausting to want something this much and not be able to make it happen.
  • The two-week wait. After your fertile window, the wait to know can feel endless. Every twinge becomes a question. Many women over-read early symptoms and then feel crushed when the period comes. It is one of the cruellest parts, and it is not a sign of weakness that you find it hard.
  • The comparison. Every pregnancy announcement, every baby shower, every "so when is it your turn" can land like a small wound. Feeling that does not make you a bad friend or an ungrateful person. It makes you human.
  • The family pressure. The questions, the advice, the well-meant prayers that somehow add weight, the comments about "wasting time." In our culture this can be relentless, and it can come from people who genuinely love you.
  • The strain on the two of you. When sex becomes a task on a schedule, closeness can quietly drain out of it. Trying can make partners feel like teammates on a project instead of lovers.

Steadier ways through

  • Protect your intimacy. Keep some closeness that has nothing to do with timing or trying: time together, touch, laughter, a date with no baby-talk allowed. Not every moment has to be about conceiving, and your marriage is worth protecting for its own sake.
  • Handle the two-week wait gently. Fill it with normal life where you can. Try not to test too early, which usually just adds heartbreak. Line up something kind for yourself around the time your period is due, whichever way it goes.
  • Set boundaries with family. You are allowed to say, kindly but firmly, "We would rather not talk about this right now. We will share news when we have it." A few ready lines help: "We are trusting God's timing and would love your prayers, not questions." You do not owe anyone updates on your body.
  • Turn down the noise. Step back from the endless searching and the forums that leave you more frightened at 2am. Bring your real questions here, to the community, or to your doctor.
  • Lean on women who understand. The protected community exists for exactly this. Say the thing you cannot say out loud anywhere else, to women who will not flinch.
  • Watch your own wellbeing. If the low or anxious feelings are heavy, if you are losing interest in life, or they are simply not lifting, please reach out, to a doctor, a counsellor, or the MANI line on 0809 111 6264. Carrying this does not have to break you.

Faith, hope, and getting help

For many of us, faith carries us through this waiting, and prayer is real medicine for the heart. Hold on to that. And know this too: trusting God and seeing a doctor are not opposites. Seeking help is not a lack of faith, any more than taking medicine for malaria is. Faith and good medical care walk together. If anyone makes you feel that getting checked means you do not believe enough, gently set that voice aside. Wisdom is also a gift, and using it is faith in action.

If you have known loss

If you have experienced a pregnancy loss, we are so sorry. Grief after loss is real, it is not "just one of those things," and it deserves gentleness and, where you want it, real support and counselling. We will not offer you ways to "prevent" a loss, because that would be dishonest and unkind, and most losses are nobody's fault. What we will do is hold space for the grief and point you firmly to the people who can truly help you through it.

If it has been a long time

If you have been on this road for years, not months, this page is for you especially.

First, you are not a failure, and you have not been forgotten. Long journeys are more common than the quiet around them suggests, and many of the women you see with children today walked a longer road than anyone ever knew.

A few gentle things.

  • Make sure you have both been properly checked. If it has been a long time and you have not had a full assessment for both of you, that is the next step. A specialist can look for reasons that simple trying cannot fix, and there are more options now than there used to be.
  • Protect your wellbeing fiercely. Long waits wear down even strong women and strong marriages. Please lean on the community, on people who love you, and on real support if the weight is heavy. You matter, whatever happens.
  • You are allowed to grieve and to hope at the same time. You do not have to be endlessly positive, and you do not have to give up. Both can be true in the same week.
  • Explore your options honestly. Treatments like IVF, and paths like adoption, are not defeats. They are ways many families are quietly built. Going to learn about them costs you nothing but a conversation.

Whatever this journey holds, your worth was never tied to it. You are already whole.

Bonus · When There's Something More

Sometimes trying uncovers a condition, or you already live with one. This is a plain-words guide to the common ones, so the words are less frightening and you know who to see. It explains and points you to the right care. It never tries to treat any of these, and it never promises an outcome.

  • PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome). A common hormone condition that can cause irregular or absent periods, extra hair growth, weight changes, and difficulty with ovulation. It is common and it is manageable with the right medical support. If your periods are very irregular, ask your doctor about it rather than guessing online.
  • Endometriosis. A condition where tissue like the womb lining grows outside the womb, often causing very painful, heavy periods and sometimes pain during sex. It can affect fertility. Very painful periods are worth taking to a doctor, not quietly enduring for years, which is sadly common.
  • Blocked tubes or past infection. Previous pelvic infections or surgery can sometimes affect the tubes. A doctor can check this if it is relevant to you.
  • Recurrent pregnancy loss. Losing more than one pregnancy is not something to face alone, and it is not your fault. There are specialists who investigate why and support you through it.
  • Assisted options (like IVF and IUI). If you reach the point of exploring treatments, a fertility specialist explains what each involves, the real chances, and the costs, honestly. Going to ask is a brave, sensible step, not a shameful last resort. Many families you know quietly took this road.

For any of these, the move is the same: see a doctor or a specialist. The Fertility Appointment Questions help you ask well and remember the answers.

Fertility myths, and what is actually true

Few areas of health carry as many myths as this one, and they cause real pain and wasted money. Here are some common ones, gently corrected. Where a myth touches your own situation, your doctor is the person to trust.

  • "If you just relax, it will happen." Reducing stress is good for you, but stress is not why most couples take time, and telling a woman to "just relax" usually makes it worse. Please do not carry this blame.
  • "It is always the woman's problem." Untrue. Up to about half of the picture is on the man's side, which is exactly why Part 3 matters.
  • "Certain positions or standing on your head after sex help." There is no good evidence for special positions or acrobatics. Regular sex around your fertile window is what counts.
  • "Fertility tea will fix it." No tea or mixture can promise conception, and some can harm you. Save your money and your health.
  • "You should have sex every single day." Not necessary. Every one to two days in the window is plenty, and daily pressure can strain your relationship.
  • "Irregular periods mean you can never conceive." Irregular cycles are worth seeing a doctor about, but many women with irregular periods do conceive, sometimes with simple help.
  • "You are too old at 30." Fertility declines gradually with age and more from the mid-30s, but the "cliff at 30" idea is exaggerated and cruel. Age is one factor, not a sentence.

The theme is simple. Trust real research and your own doctor, not the loudest voice at the party or the boldest advert online.

Quick answers to questions women ask

How long should we try before seeing a doctor?

About a year if you are under 35, about six months if you are 35 or older, and sooner if your periods are irregular or you have a known condition. Earlier is always fine if you are worried.

Is it my fault? Am I doing something wrong?

Almost certainly not. Conception depends on many things, and up to half of the picture is on the man's side. Blaming yourself is common, and it is rarely fair or accurate.

Do we have to have sex every day?

No. Every one to two days across your fertile window is plenty. Some couples just enjoy each other every couple of days all month, with no schedule at all, which is often kinder on the relationship.

Are fertility teas and herbal mixtures safe?

Be careful. Many are untested, some can be unsafe, and none can promise anything. Ask your doctor or pharmacist before taking anything herbal, especially with other medicine.

Can stress stop me from conceiving?

Stress is hard on you and your relationship, and lowering it is good for both. But please do not blame yourself for being stressed, and do not let anyone tell you that you are "just trying too hard." That is not how it works.

I feel awful when friends announce pregnancies. Is that normal?

Completely. It does not make you a bad person. If those feelings are heavy or lasting, reach out for support; you do not have to pretend to be fine.

Can I still conceive with irregular periods or PCOS?

Many women do, sometimes with a doctor's help. Irregular cycles are a good reason to get checked rather than to lose hope.

Should we both get checked, or just me?

Both. Because up to half of the picture is on the man's side, checking only the woman misses half the answers.

How soon can I test for pregnancy?

Testing too early often gives a false disappointment. Waiting until your period is due, or a day or two after, gives a more reliable answer and saves some heartache.

We have one child already but cannot conceive again. Is that a real thing?

Yes. Difficulty conceiving again after a first child is real and has a name (secondary). It is worth seeing a doctor about, and you deserve the same support and care as anyone else.

Is it too late for me?

Age is one factor, not a full stop. If you are worried about time, that is a reason to see a doctor sooner rather than to lose hope. Let a professional give you your real picture.

Everyone gives me different advice. Who do I listen to?

Trust your own doctor for medical questions, and bring the rest here or to the community. You can thank people for their advice and still do things your way.
