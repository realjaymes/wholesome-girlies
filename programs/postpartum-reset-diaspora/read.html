Welcome

You just had a baby. So let us start with the question nobody remembered to ask. How are you?

For nine months, people checked on you. Then the baby comes, everyone turns to the baby, and you are left to work out the rest by yourself, on almost no sleep.

Your body does not feel like yours yet. Your marriage feels different. And some days you do not feel like yourself, and you cannot tell if that is normal.

This is the plan that puts you first. Not the baby. Not the woman you were before. You.

We built it around the three things birth changes that nobody warns you about. Your body. Your marriage. Your mind. It does not try to sort out the baby. It looks after you.

How to use this

You do not have to start at the beginning. Open the part you need today. If your body is the biggest worry this week, start there. If it is you and your partner drifting apart, start there. The rest will wait.

Take one small thing at a time. You are tired. Read a little. Try one thing. Come back later. Nothing here runs out.

Go at your own speed. There is no "bounce back" plan in here. No timeline. No number on a scale. "Better" means feeling strong, steady, and like yourself again. Whatever that looks like for you is enough.

Keep your doctor close. This helps you understand what is normal and ask better questions. It does not replace your nurse, your doctor, or your check-ups. Anywhere something needs real care, we tell you plainly.

Your safety first

Before anything else, here are the signs that mean stop and get help.

Call your doctor or go to a clinic now if you have:

  • Very heavy bleeding. You soak a full pad in an hour, or you pass big clots.
  • A fever, or you feel hot, shivery, and unwell.
  • Strong pain, or pain on one side that is getting worse.
  • A hot, swollen, painful spot on your leg.
  • Trouble breathing, or chest pain.
  • A very bad headache with blurred sight.
  • A cut or wound that is opening, smelling, or leaking.

For your mind: if you feel low, worried, or not yourself for more than two weeks, please talk to someone trained to help. Sooner if the feeling is heavy. If you ever think about harming yourself or your baby, treat it as an emergency and get help straight away. In Nigeria you can call the Mentally Aware Nigeria Initiative on 0809 111 6264.

The full list is at the back, in the "When to See a Doctor" guide. Keep that page. Your safety never depends on you finishing this program.

Your six weeks, gently

Everyone told you about the birth. Almost nobody told you about the weeks after. So here is a gentle map of the first six weeks, and a little beyond.

This is not a strict plan you must keep up with. Some weeks you will do a lot. Some weeks you will just get through. Both are fine. Think of it as a guide to what usually matters most, and when. Your body and your baby set the real pace, and your doctor's advice always comes first.

Pillar 1 · Your Body

Your body did something huge. It is allowed to take time, and it is allowed to feel strange for a while. This part helps you understand what is happening, what is normal, and what needs a doctor. When you know what to expect, the fear gets quieter, and you can heal without rushing or hurting yourself.

One rule sits over everything here. Check with your doctor before you start any exercise or tummy work after birth. This is even more important after a caesarean (an operation to deliver the baby) or a hard birth. This is learning, not a workout plan.

What heals when

Recovery is not one thing that happens on one day. Different parts of you heal at different speeds. Knowing roughly what to expect helps you feel calmer.

This is a general guide, not a timetable you must match. Every body and every birth is different.

Healing down below

If you had a normal (vaginal) birth, you may have a tear, or a small cut called an episiotomy, with stitches. This area is sore, and it heals over days and weeks. A few simple things help a lot.

  • Keep it clean. Pour warm water over the area while you wee, using a small jug or cup. Then pat dry gently, from front to back. Change your pad often.
  • Ease the soreness. Sitting in a few inches of warm water (a salt bath or sitz bath) can soothe it. In the first days, a cool pack wrapped in a soft, clean cloth can help too.
  • Take the pressure off. Lying down rests the area. When you sit, a soft cushion helps.
  • The stitches dissolve on their own. You do not need to have them removed.

The first poo after birth scares many women, especially with stitches. It is almost always fine. To make it easier: drink plenty of water, eat fruit, vegetables, and other foods with fibre, and do not strain. You can hold a clean pad of tissue gently against the stitches while you go, for comfort. If you get very constipated, ask a pharmacist or your doctor for something gentle and safe.

Call your doctor if the soreness gets worse instead of better, or if the area becomes hot, swollen, or starts to smell. That can be a sign of infection.

Other changes nobody warns you about

Birth changes more than you expect. Here are some common ones, so they do not catch you off guard.

  • Cramps while you feed (afterpains). In the first days, your womb cramps as it shrinks back to size, and feeding can make it stronger. It is a good sign, and it usually settles within a week.
  • Sweating at night. You may wake up soaked. This is your body letting go of the extra water from pregnancy. It is normal and passes in a few weeks. Keep water by the bed.
  • Piles (haemorrhoids). These are swollen veins around your back passage. They are very common after birth and can be sore or itchy. Fibre, water, not straining, and a cream from the pharmacy usually help. They often settle in a few weeks. Tell your doctor if they bleed a lot or do not improve.
  • Your period coming back. There is no fixed time. If you breastfeed a lot, it often stays away longer. The first one may be heavier, or different from before. That is usually normal.
  • Swollen feet and legs. Some swelling in the first days is common as your body clears extra fluid. But sudden swelling, or swelling on one side with pain, redness, or heat, needs a doctor quickly.

Rebuilding your core safely

After birth, many mums notice their tummy feels soft, and that the middle seems to bulge when they sit up. This is very common. It has a name: diastasis recti. It just means the two muscles that run down the front of your tummy have moved apart to make room for the baby. This leaves a gap. It is normal. You did nothing wrong. For most women it gets better with time and gentle movement.

Two things matter here.

First, notice it, but do not try to judge it yourself. You may feel a soft gap above or below your belly button. It is good to notice. But a doctor or a women's-health physiotherapist (a therapist who helps with muscles after birth) can check how wide the gap is and tell you what your body needs. If the gap is not closing, or you feel a bulge, pain, or weakness, ask to see one.

Second, gentle comes before hard. The early weeks are for slow, easy movement, not sit-ups. Sit-ups, planks, and lifting heavy things too soon can make the gap worse, not better.

A safe place to start, once you feel ready, is simple belly breathing:

  • Sit or lie down comfortably.
  • Breathe in slowly through your nose and let your tummy rise.
  • Breathe out slowly through your mouth. As you do, gently draw your tummy in, as if hugging the baby from inside.
  • Do this a few times. No straining. It should feel easy.

That is it. This gentle breathing wakes up the deep muscles first. When your doctor says you are ready for more, build up slowly from there. Slow is not falling behind. Slow is how it heals.

There is no crash plan in here on purpose. Rushing your body is how women get hurt. Steady is stronger.

If you had a caesarean

A caesarean is a birth and a big operation on your tummy. Your recovery deserves that respect. Give yourself more time, not less.

  • Look after your cut. Keep it clean and dry. Wear soft, high underwear that sits above the cut, not on it. When you cough, laugh, or feed, hold a small pillow against your tummy for support. Itching or numb skin around the cut is normal as it heals.
  • Move gently, and slower than after a normal birth. Do not lift anything heavier than your baby, and do not do any tummy work, until your doctor says it is fine. To get out of bed, roll onto your side first, then push up with your arms. This protects the cut.
  • Call your doctor if the cut becomes hot, red, swollen, more painful, or starts to leak or smell, or if you have a fever. These can be signs of an infection, and an infection needs care quickly.

You did not "take the easy way." You healed from an operation while looking after a newborn. Be as kind to yourself as you would be to a friend.

The pelvic floor, and the leaking nobody mentions

Your pelvic floor is the group of muscles at the bottom of your tummy. They hold up your bladder, your womb, and your back passage, like a hammock. Pregnancy and birth stretch these muscles. So it is very common to leak a little wee when you cough, sneeze, laugh, or lift something in the early weeks.

Nobody warns you about this. Then it feels shameful and lonely. You are not broken. And you are far from the only one.

Gentle squeezes help many women build these muscles back up. Here is a simple one, when your body feels ready:

  • Imagine you are trying to stop yourself from weeing, and from passing wind, at the same time.
  • Squeeze those muscles and lift them up. Hold for a few seconds.
  • Then let go fully and rest.
  • Do a few at a time. A good trick is to do them every time you feed the baby, so you remember.

But leaking is not something you must just live with forever. See a doctor or a women's-health physiotherapist if:

  • The leaking carries on past the early weeks.
  • You leak stool, or cannot control wind.
  • You feel heaviness or something bulging in your vagina.
  • Sex is painful when you go back to it.

These problems are common, they can be treated, and you deserve help with them, not a life spent quietly working around them.

Breastfeeding comfort, for your body

This part is about your body, not about whether the baby is getting enough. Feeding can be much harder and more painful than the videos make it look. The pain is worth taking seriously.

Painful latch. "Latch" means the way the baby takes the breast into their mouth. A little tenderness in the first days is normal. But sharp pain the whole feed, or cracked and bleeding nipples, usually means the latch needs fixing. Good news: it can be fixed.

  • Signs the latch may be off: pain right through the feed, a nipple that comes out flattened or pinched, clicking sounds, the baby slipping off often.
  • Signs it is going well: it may be tender at first but eases, you can hear soft swallowing, the baby's mouth is wide and covers more than just the nipple.
  • A nurse or a breastfeeding helper can watch a feed and fix it in minutes. Ask early. The community can point you to help.

When your breasts get very full and hard (engorgement). When your milk comes in, your breasts can feel rock-hard, hot, and sore. To ease it: feed often, use gentle warmth just before a feed to help the milk flow, and use something cool afterward to calm the soreness. Soft hand-expressing of a little milk can take the edge off if you are too full.

Worried you are not making enough milk. Many mums fear this. Usually, a baby who is putting on weight and wetting enough nappies is getting enough. If you are worried, ask a nurse early, instead of carrying the worry alone.

Get help quickly if you have a hot, red, painful patch on your breast with a fever or feeling unwell. This can be a breast infection called mastitis, and it needs treatment. Also get help if the pain is so bad you cannot feed at all.

However you end up feeding your baby, both a fed baby and a mother who is coping matter. There is no prize for suffering.

Feeding your baby, your way

However you feed your baby, you are a good mother. Breast, bottle, formula, or a mix, what matters is a fed baby and a mum who is coping.

  • Breastfeeding is worth support if you want it, and the latch help above can make it far easier. Feeding very often in the early days, sometimes every hour in the evening, is normal. It is called cluster feeding. It is the baby building your milk supply, not you failing.
  • Formula or bottle feeding is a safe and loving choice. Follow the tin's instructions carefully so feeds are clean and correctly mixed. It also lets other people share feeds, which can protect your sleep.
  • Mixing breast and bottle works well for many families too.

Do not let anyone shame you for how you feed. It is your body, your baby, and your choice. If feeding of any kind is making you miserable or unwell, that is a good reason to get help and look at your options, not to suffer quietly.

Hair, skin, and eating to heal

Your hair. Around two to four months after birth, many women lose a lot of hair. You may see it in the comb or the shower drain and feel afraid. It is the pregnancy hormones settling down. It is normal, it is temporary, and for most women the hair grows back. Be gentle with it and try not to panic.

Your skin. Dry skin, spots, dark patches, and stretch marks slowly fading from red to silver are all normal as your hormones settle. Kindness and time do more than any costly cream.

Eating to heal, not to shrink. This is not the time for a diet. Your body is healing. If you are breastfeeding, it is feeding two. Aim for regular, simple meals and plenty of water.

  • Good, easy choices: beans, eggs, fish, moi moi, chicken, vegetable soups with plenty of green leaves, pap, fruit, groundnuts.
  • Iron helps you rebuild blood and fight tiredness: liver, red meat, beans, dark green vegetables like ugu and spinach.
  • Keep snacks you can eat with one hand near where you feed, so you actually eat.
  • Drink a cup of water every time you sit down to feed.

Done beats perfect. A simple meal you finish is worth more than a fancy one you never make.

Rest, when you can get it

You cannot pour from an empty cup. Rest is not a treat. It is part of healing.

  • Sleep when the baby sleeps, even once a day. The washing can wait.
  • Let visitors help, or let them come another day. You do not owe anyone a hosting.
  • Share the nights if you can. Even one feed taken by someone else can give you a longer stretch of sleep.
  • When someone asks "what can I do?", have a real answer ready: cook, wash plates, hold the baby while I bathe or sleep.

If you have family doing omugwo, let them carry the house so you can carry yourself. That is exactly what it is for.

Surviving on broken sleep

Broken sleep is one of the hardest parts of these weeks, and it makes everything else feel worse. You cannot fix it fully right now, but you can soften it.

  • Sleep when the baby sleeps, even for twenty minutes. It counts.
  • Share the nights if there is anyone to share them with. One person does a stretch while the other sleeps, then you swap.
  • If you breastfeed, someone else can still bring the baby to you, then change and settle them, so you do less.
  • Let everything else drop. A tidy house does not matter this month. Your rest does.
  • Go easy on your phone in the night. The bright light wakes your brain up more.

It does not last forever, even though it feels like it will. It gets better.

Pillar 2 · Your Marriage

Somewhere between the night feeds and the healing, many couples stop feeling like a couple. If that is you, you are not failing. Your marriage is not broken. It is carrying something new and heavy, on no sleep, and nobody gave either of you a map. This part is the map.

Why you stopped feeling like a couple

Let us name it plainly, because naming it takes away some of its power.

Maybe you argue more, over small things that are really about being tired and unseen. Maybe you barely talk, and it feels like you are two tired people running shifts, not partners. Maybe you feel quietly upset that so much of it falls on you. You are the one healing, feeding, and awake at night, and it does not feel shared. Maybe you want to feel close but do not know how to say so without it turning into a fight.

All of this is common in the first months. It does not mean you chose the wrong person. It means you are both worn out and pointing that tiredness at each other, because you cannot blame the baby.

The way back is not one big, dramatic talk. It is small, honest steps, repeated.

Finding your way back

  • Make "close" small on purpose. Right now, closeness can be ten minutes with tea, a hand on a shoulder, or one real question that is not about the baby. It does not have to be a night out you are both too tired to enjoy.
  • Ask for exact help, not "more help." "Please take the 5am feed on Saturday so I can sleep" works far better than "you never help." Being exact is kind. It is also easier to say yes to.
  • Say the quiet thing gently. "I feel like I have disappeared" or "I miss us" is more honest, and lands better, than a list of everything he did wrong. It invites him in instead of putting him on trial.

Here are a few things you can actually say, word for word:

  • "I am not upset with you. I am just very tired and I need us to be a team tonight."
  • "Can you take the baby for one hour so I can sleep or bathe? That would help me more than anything."
  • "I do not need you to fix it. I just need you to sit with me for a bit."
  • "I want us to feel close again. Can we start small?"

About sex and closeness. Your body needs time to heal first, usually until after your check-up, and often longer. Even after the doctor says your body is ready, wanting sex can take much longer. That is completely normal, and it is not a rejection of him. There is no timetable you owe anyone.

When you are ready, start with closeness that is not sex: holding hands, a hug, sitting together, being on the same team again. If sex hurts when you go back to it, that is common and it can be treated, so please tell a doctor instead of forcing yourself through the pain.

When he does not seem to understand

Sometimes it feels like he just does not get it, and that hurts. Often it is not that he does not care. It is that he cannot see what you carry, and nobody taught him either.

  • Show him, do not only tell him. The Partner Brief below puts it in plain words he can take in.
  • Ask for one thing at a time, and let him get it right, instead of handing him a long list when you are both tired.
  • Notice and thank the help he does give, even when it is not perfect. People do more of what they feel seen for.
  • Save the bigger talks for calm moments, not 2am when you are both empty.

If he is truly unwilling to help, or if you ever feel unsafe, that is bigger than tiredness. Please talk to someone you trust, and reach out for support.

When family adds pressure

In our culture, family come with love and with a lot of opinions. That can be a gift and a weight at the same time.

  • It is okay to say no to advice you did not ask for. "Thank you, we are handling it our way" is a full answer.
  • You and your partner should agree on the important things privately first, then face family as one team. It is much harder for anyone to push between you when you stand together.
  • Accept the practical help, and gently set the rest aside. You can be grateful and still make your own choices.

The Partner and Family Brief

Some of what you carry is invisible. People who love you cannot help with what they cannot see. So hand them this. Copy the part that fits, send it in a message, or read it out.

For your partner

Here is what is really going on for me, so we can be on the same team.

  • My body is healing from something big. It takes weeks, not days. Some things I cannot do yet, even when I look fine.
  • I am more tired than I have ever been. It is not drama, and it is not about you.
  • The most useful things you can do: take a whole feed or a whole shift so I can sleep, do a chore without being asked, and tell me I am doing well. That last one truly matters.
  • Please do not comment on my body or how fast I am "getting back." I need to feel safe in my body, not judged.
  • If I go quiet or start crying, just sit with me. You do not have to fix it. I mostly need to not be alone in it.

For visiting family

We are so grateful you came. The kindest help right now is practical: cook, clean, and hold the baby so she can bathe or sleep. Please let her rest without feeling watched. And please hold back advice about her body or her feeding unless she asks. She is doing beautifully. Looking after her is looking after the baby.

Pillar 3 · Your Mind

This part is gentle, and it is important. Everything here is learning and support. It is never a diagnosis and never treatment. Where your mind needs real care, we will always point you to it. Because that matters just as much as your body.

The rollercoaster of feelings, explained

In the first days and weeks, big feelings that change fast are very common. Crying for no clear reason. Feeling flat, then fine, then flooded. Soft and full of love one hour, snappy the next.

This is often called the baby blues. It usually builds in the first week and then eases over the following weeks. Your hormones are changing a lot, you are exhausted, and your whole life turned upside down overnight.

Feeling this way does not mean something is wrong with you. And it does not mean you do not love your baby. Two things can be true at once. You would do anything for this child, and this is one of the hardest things you have ever done.

Baby blues, or something more

The baby blues lift. When the low or worried feelings are heavier, or they last, it can be something more: postpartum depression (deep, lasting low mood after birth) or postpartum anxiety (constant, strong worry after birth). Both are common. And, importantly, both can be helped with the right support.

This is not about labelling yourself. It is about knowing when to reach out, because you deserve to feel better, and you do not have to earn it.

Here is a plain way to tell them apart. This is a guide only, not a diagnosis.

More likely just baby blues:

  • Starts in the first days.
  • Comes and goes.
  • Eases by around two weeks.
  • You can still feel moments of joy and get through your day.

Worth reaching out about:

  • You feel low, empty, or worried most of the day, most days.
  • It lasts longer than two weeks.
  • You cannot sleep even when the baby sleeps.
  • You lose interest in things and people you used to enjoy.
  • You feel constant dread, or your mind races with worry.
  • You feel like a bad mother, or that your baby would be better off without you.

If the second list sounds like you, that is not weakness, and it is not your fault. Please talk to a doctor or a trained counsellor. Getting help early works, and many, many women do it. In Nigeria you can call the Mentally Aware Nigeria Initiative on 0809 111 6264.

If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, treat it as an emergency and get help immediately.

What getting help can look like

Reaching out can feel like a big scary step, so here is what it can really be:

  • Telling your own doctor at your check-up, "I have not been feeling like myself." They will not judge you. They hear this often.
  • Calling a helpline like the MANI line above, just to talk to someone trained.
  • Talking to a counsellor. Sometimes a few sessions make a real difference.
  • Sometimes a doctor may suggest treatment. It is safe, common, and there are options that work with breastfeeding. You would be in good company.

You are not "making a fuss." You are looking after the person your baby needs most.

Scary thoughts that pop in

Many mums get sudden, frightening thoughts or pictures in their mind. A thought that something bad could happen to the baby. A scary "what if" you would never want. Then you feel horrified and guilty for even thinking it.

Please hear this clearly. These thoughts are common, especially with tiredness and worry. Having them does not mean you would ever act on them. Usually, being so upset by the thought is a sign of how much you love and want to protect your baby, not the opposite.

You do not have to carry this in shame. Saying it out loud, to a partner, a friend, the community, or a professional, usually takes away a lot of its power.

Reach out for help if these thoughts come very often, if they start to feel like urges rather than fears, if you cannot put them down, or if they stop you living normally. Talk to a doctor, or call the MANI line on 0809 111 6264. Help works, and you deserve it.

Getting yourself back

  • Guilt is not proof you are failing. Feeling guilty usually means you care, not that you are doing badly. You are allowed to find this hard.
  • Make "self-care" small and real. Not spa days. A hot meal you eat sitting down. Ten minutes outside in the sun. A proper bath. A voice note to a friend. Small and real beats big and impossible.
  • Do not compare your inside to someone else's outside. The mums who look perfect online are having hard nights too. Nobody posts those.
  • Let people in. The mums who feel steadiest are rarely the ones doing it alone. Lean on the community. Ask the question you are almost too tired to type. That is exactly what it is there for.
  • You are still in there. The woman you were has not vanished. She is tired and stretched around a new life. She comes back, bit by bit, and then more.

A gentle check-in

Not a test, and there is no score. Just questions to sit with honestly, every week or two.

  • Can I feel small moments of joy or calm?
  • Am I sleeping when I get the chance, or lying awake worried?
  • Do I feel connected to my baby and the people around me, or cut off?
  • Can I get through the day, most days?
  • Are the heavy feelings slowly easing, or getting heavier?

If your honest answers worry you, or a few of them have felt wrong for more than two weeks, take that as your sign to reach out. To a doctor, a counsellor, or the MANI line on 0809 111 6264. Reaching out early is strength, not failure. You matter here, not only as this baby's mother.

There is also a private Gentle Mind Check-in on Wholesome Girlies, a quiet place to come back to whenever you need it.

Going back to work, or leaving your baby

In many families, maternity leave is short. Going back to work, or leaving your baby with someone else, can come sooner than you feel ready for. This part helps you get ready, in body and in mind.

The feelings are normal. Guilt, worry, sadness, and even a little relief, often all at once. Leaving your baby does not make you a bad mother. Most mums feel this, and it eases with time and a routine.

Get practical, in good time.

  • Sort out who will care for your baby. Let your baby spend a little time with them before your first day, so it is not all new at once.
  • If you breastfeed and want to keep going, you can express and store your milk. As a rough guide, breastmilk keeps for a few hours at room temperature, a few days in the fridge, and longer in the freezer, in clean, labelled containers. Ask a nurse for the exact times, and build up a small store in the weeks before you go back.
  • Do a practice run. A short time apart before the real day helps you both get used to it.
  • Ask your workplace about a clean, private place and short breaks to express, if you need them.

On the day, and after.

  • Pack everything the night before. Mornings are calmer that way.
  • A warm, short goodbye is kinder to you both than a long, tearful one.
  • Check in with yourself over the next few weeks. If the low or worried feelings are heavy or do not ease, treat it like the mind section says, and reach out.

You are still their mother every hour you are away. Going back to work does not change that.

Bonus · When to See a Doctor

Keep this page. Screenshot it, print it, or save it. It is your safety net. It stays yours even if you decide this program is not for you.

This does not replace your own gut feeling. If something feels seriously wrong, get help even if it is not on this list.

Your body: get help now

  • Very heavy bleeding: soaking a full pad in an hour, or passing big clots.
  • Bleeding that had eased, then turns bright-red and heavy again.
  • A fever, or feeling hot, shivery, and unwell.
  • Strong pain, pain on one side, or pain that is getting worse.
  • A red, hot, sore patch on your breast with feeling unwell (this can be the breast infection, mastitis).
  • A caesarean or other cut that is red, hot, swollen, more painful, opening, leaking, or smelling.
  • Pain, swelling, redness, or heat in your leg or calf.
  • Trouble breathing, chest pain, or a racing heart.
  • A very bad headache, changes in your sight, or swelling in your face and hands.
  • Leaking wee or stool that will not settle, or a feeling of something bulging down below.

Your mind: reach out

  • Feeling low, empty, or very worried most of the day for more than two weeks.
  • Cannot sleep even when the baby sleeps, or you have constant dread and racing thoughts.
  • Feeling cut off from your baby, or that you are failing.
  • Any thought of harming yourself or your baby, or that your family would be better without you. This is an emergency. Get help immediately.

In Nigeria: Mentally Aware Nigeria Initiative, 0809 111 6264. For a body emergency, go to your nearest hospital or call your doctor or clinic. Wherever you are, your nurse, doctor, or emergency service comes first. We would always rather you go and get care than wait.

Quick answers to questions mums ask

When can I start exercising?

Gentle walking, and the easy breathing in this guide, can start early, whenever you feel up to it. For anything more, wait for your check-up and your doctor's okay, then start slow. After a caesarean, wait longer.

Is it normal to still look pregnant?

Yes. Your womb takes about six weeks to shrink back, and your tummy takes longer still. It is normal, and there is nothing to rush.

When will I feel like myself again?

There is no fixed date, and it usually comes back bit by bit, not all at once. Most women feel steadier over weeks and months. If you feel worse over time, or stuck in a low place, please reach out.

I do not feel a rush of love for my baby. Is something wrong with me?

No. For many mums, love grows over days and weeks instead of arriving all at once. This is far more common than people admit. If low feelings are heavy or lasting, talk to someone. But a bond that grows slowly, on its own, is normal.

When can we have sex again?

Usually after your check-up, once your body has healed, and only when you feel ready, which is often later than the doctor's okay. If it hurts, tell a doctor. There is no rush and no rule you owe anyone.

How do I know if I am bleeding too much?

Soaking a full pad in an hour, or passing big clots, is too much, so get help. Bleeding that had settled and then turns heavy and bright-red again also needs a call.

Can I take medicine for pain while breastfeeding?

Some pain relief is safe while breastfeeding and some is not. Ask a pharmacist or your doctor which is right for you, instead of guessing.

My baby cries so much. Is that normal?

A lot of crying in the early weeks is common, and it is covered fully in the next stage, The First Baby Playbook. If your baby seems unwell, is not feeding or wetting nappies, or your gut says something is wrong, always call a doctor.

Everyone gives me different advice. Who do I listen to?

Trust your own doctor for medical questions, and trust yourself for your baby. You can thank people for their advice and still do things your way.
