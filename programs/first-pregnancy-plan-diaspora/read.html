Welcome

You saw two lines on a test. Then the joy arrived, and right behind it came a thousand questions.

Is this cramp normal? Should I feel this tired? Is it a bad sign that the nausea stopped? Nobody hands you a manual with the pregnancy. Your clinic visit is weeks away, and the questions will not wait that long. So you Google at 2am, and Google gives you the worst answer every time. Then everyone has advice. Your mother says one thing, the group chat says another, the aunty at church swears by something else, and half of it disagrees with the other half.

Take a breath. You are not doing this wrong, and you are not doing it alone.

This is one calm plan for the whole nine months, instead of a pile of advice that all points in different directions. It walks with you week by week, from your positive test to delivery day. It helps you understand what is happening to your body and your baby, tell what is normal from what needs a call, eat without second-guessing every meal, understand what your scans and tests are actually for, and get ready for the birth and the first days.

What it will not do is diagnose you, read your results for you, or promise you anything about the birth. No guide can. Your antenatal care comes first, and we support around it. Real information, never false comfort.

How to use this

You do not have to start at the beginning. Open the part you need today. If you are early and just want to know what is happening in there, start with the week-by-week part. If a symptom is worrying you tonight, go straight to "Is This Normal." The rest will wait.

Take one small thing at a time. Read a little. Try one thing. Come back later. Nothing here runs out, and it is yours to keep for the whole pregnancy.

Go at your own pace. There is no test at the end, and no gold star for finishing fast. Some weeks you will read a lot. Some weeks you will just get through, and that is fine too.

Keep your care close. This helps you understand what is normal and ask better questions. It never replaces your midwife, your doctor, or your antenatal check-ups. A quick word before we go further: antenatal just means your pregnancy check-ups, the visits where a midwife or doctor looks after you and the baby before the birth. Anywhere something needs real care, we tell you plainly, and more than once where it matters.

Your safety first

Before anything else, here are the things that mean stop and get help. Pregnancy is mostly a healthy, normal time, but a few signs should never be talked yourself out of, not by Google, not by an aunty, and not by us.

Contact your doctor, midwife, or maternity unit straight away if you have:

  • Any bleeding from your vagina. At any stage, any amount, contact your maternity unit or doctor immediately. Do not wait to see if it stops. This is not a "usually it's fine" situation, and we will never tell you it is. Getting checked is always the right move.
  • Any change in your baby's movements once you have started to feel them (usually from around the middle of pregnancy). Fewer movements, slower movements, or movements that feel different from your baby's normal pattern all mean you contact your maternity unit the same day, every time. Never wait until morning, never wait for your next appointment, and never let anyone talk you out of going. This one matters more than almost anything else in this guide.
  • A bad headache that will not go, especially with blurred sight, spots in front of your eyes, or sudden swelling in your face, hands, or feet.
  • Strong pain in your tummy, or pain high up under your ribs.
  • A high temperature, or feeling hot, shivery, and unwell.
  • Being sick so much that you cannot keep any food or water down.
  • Pain, burning, or a strong smell when you wee.
  • Your waters breaking, or a sudden gush or trickle of fluid, before your due time.

For your mind: if you feel low, worried, or not yourself for more than two weeks, please talk to someone trained to help. Sooner if the feeling is heavy. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, treat it as an emergency and get help straight away. In Nigeria you can call the Mentally Aware Nigeria Initiative (MANI) on 0809 111 6264.

Keep this page. Screenshot it, so it is on your phone even without data. Your safety never depends on you finishing this program, and we would always rather you get checked and be told all is well than wait and worry.

Pillar 1 · Know What's Happening (Week by Week)

Most of the fear in a first pregnancy comes from one thing: not knowing what is normal, or what is coming next. This part answers that. When you understand what is happening to your body and your baby at each stage, the surprises get smaller and the worry gets quieter.

Pregnancy is usually counted in three stages called trimesters. Each one is about three months long, and each has its own feel. Here is the gentle map, so you know roughly where you are and what tends to matter most.

Your first trimester (roughly weeks 1 to 13)

This is the hidden stage. On the outside you may look exactly the same, but inside, an enormous amount is happening very fast. Your baby grows from a tiny cluster of cells into a form with a beating heart, the beginnings of a face, and little limbs, all in these first weeks.

For you, this is often the stage of the strong early symptoms. Tiredness that flattens you in the afternoon. Nausea, sometimes with being sick, that people call "morning sickness" even though it can come at any time of day. Sore, heavy breasts. Going off foods you used to love, or craving strange ones. Needing to wee more often. A lot of women feel worst around weeks 6 to 10, and then start to feel better as the first trimester ends.

If you feel awful, you are not weak, and it does not mean anything is wrong. If you feel almost nothing, that is also normal and not a bad sign. Bodies simply differ. The one thing to hold on to from your safety page: any bleeding, at any point, means you contact your doctor or maternity unit, even here in the early weeks.

Your second trimester (roughly weeks 14 to 27)

For many women this is the sweet spot. The early sickness often eases, some energy comes back, and you start to feel more like yourself. Your bump begins to show, so the pregnancy becomes real to the world, not just to you.

Somewhere in this stage, often between about 18 and 24 weeks for a first pregnancy, you feel your baby move for the first time. It can feel like flutters, bubbles, or a light tapping. Once you know your baby's pattern of movement, that pattern becomes one of your most important signs. From then on, any change in it means a same-day call, every time. We say this more than once in this guide on purpose, because it matters that much.

This is also the stage of the mid-pregnancy scan, and of your body changing shape in ways that can surprise you: a growing bump, a dark line down your tummy, changes to your skin. We cover the body changes in Pillar 3 and the scans in Pillar 4.

Your third trimester (roughly weeks 28 to birth)

The home stretch. Your baby is putting on weight and getting ready to be born, and your body is getting heavier and working harder. Tiredness often comes back. Sleep gets harder as the bump grows. You may get backache, swollen feet, heartburn, and more trips to the toilet as the baby presses on everything.

Towards the end, you may notice tightenings across your bump that come and go. Mild, irregular ones are often just your body practising, and they are common. But you should always know the difference between practice and the real thing, and know your warning signs, which is exactly what Pillar 2 and Pillar 5 are for.

This is the stage to get ready: pack your bag, make your birth plan, and get the few things the baby actually needs. Pillar 5 walks you through all of it, calmly and in good time.

Follow your own weeks

A general map is useful, but your pregnancy is yours. Follow it week by week with the week-by-week tracker, which tells you what is happening to you and your baby at the exact week you are in. And if you are not sure when your baby is due, the due-date tool gives you an estimate to plan around. Remember it is an estimate, not a fixed appointment. Very few babies arrive on the exact date, and that is completely normal.

Pillar 2 · Is This Normal?

This is the part you will come back to most. Pregnancy throws a lot of new feelings at your body, and the hardest question is always the same: is this just normal, or is this something I need to act on? This part helps you tell the difference, and it is very clear about the signs that should never wait.

Read the safety rules in this pillar more than once. Some things in pregnancy you can watch and wait on. A few you never do. Knowing which is which is one of the most useful things you will learn here.

The signs that always mean call now

Before the everyday symptoms, the non-negotiables. If any of these happen, you contact your doctor, midwife, or maternity unit straight away. You do not wait, you do not Google first, and you do not let anyone tell you it is probably nothing.

  • Any bleeding from your vagina, at any stage, any amount. Contact your maternity unit or doctor immediately. Not later, not tomorrow. Every time.
  • Any change in your baby's movements once you have started feeling them. Fewer, slower, or a different pattern from your baby's normal all mean a same-day call to your maternity unit, every single time. This is the one we repeat the most, because acting quickly matters so much.
  • A bad headache that will not shift, especially with blurred sight, flashing spots, or sudden swelling in your face, hands, or feet.
  • Strong or constant tummy pain, or pain high up under your ribs.
  • A high temperature or feeling hot, shivery, and unwell.
  • Being so sick you cannot keep food or water down.
  • Your waters breaking, or fluid leaking, before your due time.
  • Burning, pain, or a strong smell when you wee.

None of these are "wait and see" signs. Getting checked is always the right choice, and no one at your clinic will ever mind you coming in. That is what they are there for.

What is usually normal, stage by stage

Now the everyday things, the ones that feel alarming the first time but are usually just part of pregnancy. This is a general guide to help you feel calmer, not a rule for your exact body. When anything worries you, or does not fit what is here, ask your midwife or doctor.

In the early weeks, it is common to feel very tired, to feel sick or go off foods, to have sore breasts, to need to wee more, and to have mild, period-like cramps as your womb grows. A little white or clear discharge is normal too. Bleeding is not, so remember the rule above.

In the middle months, as the sickness eases, you may notice your bump growing, mild aches in your back and sides as things stretch, a stuffy nose, or bleeding gums when you brush. You start to feel movement. More discharge, as long as it is clear or white and not sore or smelly, is usually normal.

In the last months, expect more tiredness, heartburn, swollen feet and ankles by the end of the day, backache, trouble sleeping, and lots of trips to the toilet. Mild, irregular tightenings across the bump that come and go are often just practice. Some swelling is normal, but sudden swelling of the face and hands, with a headache, is a call-now sign from the list above.

When you are just not sure

Sometimes something feels off and it is not on any list. Trust that feeling. You know your body, and now you are learning your baby too. If your gut says something is wrong, that is a good enough reason to call your midwife or maternity unit. You will never be wasting their time. Women who trust that instinct and get checked are doing exactly the right thing, every time.

You do not have to hold all of this in your head. When something comes up that you want to raise, note it down for your next visit using the antenatal appointment questions tool, so nothing gets forgotten in the tiredness.

Pillar 3 · What Can I Eat, and How Do I Feel Human?

Almost every first-time mum worries about food. One person says avoid this, another says that is fine, and you end up scared of your own plate. This part makes it plain, and it covers the Nigerian foods you actually eat, not a list written for somebody else's kitchen. It also covers the rest of feeling human in pregnancy: moving your body, sleeping, and the changes nobody warns you about.

A gentle reminder before we start. This is general guidance to help you feel confident and ask good questions. Your midwife or doctor knows your full picture, so check anything specific with them, especially if you have a condition like diabetes.

Eating well, the simple version

You do not need special or expensive "pregnancy food." You need steady, balanced, normal meals, and Nigerian everyday food does this well.

  • Protein at meals, to help your baby grow: beans, eggs, well-cooked fish and meat, chicken, moi moi, groundnuts.
  • Green leaves and vegetables like ugu, spinach, and ewedu, for iron and folate.
  • Starchy foods for energy: rice, yam, plantain, pap, bread, potatoes.
  • Fruit across the day, washed well.
  • Dairy or its alternatives for calcium: milk, yoghurt, and pasteurised cheese.
  • Plenty of water.

You do not have to "eat for two." A little extra later in pregnancy is plenty. Aim for balance most of the time, not perfection, and do not punish yourself for a day of only being able to face bread and butter. In the sick early weeks, eating anything you can keep down is a win.

The foods to be careful with

A short, practical list, with the reasons in plain words, so it makes sense rather than just scaring you.

  • Cook meat, chicken, and fish properly. Fully cooked, not raw or pink in the middle. This avoids germs that can make you ill.
  • Avoid raw or undercooked eggs. Cook them until both the white and the yolk are firm.
  • Wash fruit and vegetables well to clear off soil and germs.
  • Fish is good for you and your baby, so keep eating it. Just do not eat huge amounts of the very large fish (like shark or swordfish) that can carry more mercury. Everyday fish, including local fish and titus, is fine in normal amounts. Cook it well.
  • Be careful with unpasteurised milk and soft mould-ripened cheeses. Pasteurised milk and firm cheeses are the safer everyday choice.
  • Avoid liver in large amounts, because too much can give more vitamin A than is good in pregnancy. A little occasionally is different from eating it often.

Your Nigerian favourites, plainly. Jollof rice, egusi, efo riro, moi moi, suya, pepper soup, and the rest are all fine when the meat or fish is cooked through and it is served hot. Suya and any grilled meat should be properly done in the middle, not still red, and eaten fresh and hot rather than sitting out. Well-cooked local fish is good for you. Spicy food is fine if your stomach can handle it, though it may make heartburn worse later on. Zobo is fine in modest amounts, but it can be very sugary, so do not drink jugs of it, and go easy if your midwife has mentioned your sugar levels.

The straight word on herbal mixtures and agbo

Be very careful with herbal mixtures, agbo, and any "cleansing" or "boosting" concoctions in pregnancy. Many are untested, the strength is never known, and some can genuinely harm you or your baby. "Natural" does not mean safe, and pregnancy is exactly the wrong time to gamble. Please do not take any herbal mixture, agbo, or traditional remedy without asking your doctor or midwife first. If someone hands you something for "the baby" or "to clean the womb," the safe answer is to check with your clinic before you drink anything. Your money and your body are better spent on the plain, proven basics above.

Coffee, and the everyday questions

  • Coffee, tea, and cola. A little is fine. It is safest to keep the caffeine modest, roughly one to two normal cups of coffee a day at most, and remember tea, cola, chocolate, and energy drinks add to the total.
  • Alcohol. The safe advice in pregnancy is none. No amount is proven safe, so it is kindest to your baby to skip it altogether.
  • Smoking. Best stopped completely, for you and the baby. Your clinic can help you if you want support to quit.

Keep the sickness bearable

If nausea and being sick are grinding you down, a few gentle things help many women: eat small amounts often rather than big meals, keep a plain snack like a biscuit by the bed for before you get up, sip water or ginger through the day, and rest when you can. If you truly cannot keep any food or water down at all, that is on your safety list, so contact your doctor rather than pushing through it alone.

Moving your body safely

Staying gently active in pregnancy is good for you, unless your doctor has told you otherwise. It can ease backache, help you sleep, lift your mood, and keep your body ready for the birth.

  • Gentle, steady movement is the aim: walking, swimming, and pregnancy-safe stretching or classes are all good.
  • Do not start a hard, brand-new, exhausting exercise plan just because you are pregnant. Keep it moderate.
  • Avoid anything with a real risk of falling or a bang to your bump, and any exercise flat on your back for long stretches later in pregnancy.
  • Stop and check with your doctor if you get pain, bleeding, dizziness, or tightenings while moving. Bleeding, remember, is always a call-now sign.

If your pregnancy has any complications, always follow your own doctor's advice on activity over this general guidance.

Sleep and the body changes nobody mentions

Sleep gets harder as the bump grows. Later in pregnancy, it is usually most comfortable, and advised, to sleep on your side rather than flat on your back, because lying flat on your back for long stretches in late pregnancy is not ideal. A pillow between your knees, and one under the bump, helps a lot. Do not lose sleep over waking in the night in a different position. It is settling to sleep on your side that matters.

The changes to your body can catch you off guard, so here are some common, normal ones: a dark line down the middle of your tummy, darker nipples, stretch marks, spots or oily skin, a fuller face, thicker hair, swollen feet by evening, and bleeding gums when you brush. Most of these settle after the baby comes. They are your body doing its job, not signs that anything is wrong. Kindness and time do more here than any costly cream.

Pillar 4 · Understand Your Scans and Tests

Pregnancy comes with scans and tests, and if nobody explains them, each one can feel like a small exam you might fail. This part takes the fear out by telling you, in plain words, what each common one is and why it is done, so you walk into your appointments knowing what is happening instead of feeling lost.

One rule sits over all of this, and it does not bend. This part explains and prepares you. It never reads or interprets your own results, and it never replaces your doctor. A number, a measurement, or a line on a scan only means something in the hands of the professional who knows your full picture. So please do not try to work out your own results from something you read online. If a result worries you, or anything is flagged, that is a conversation with your own doctor or midwife, not with a search engine.

Your antenatal check-ups

Your regular antenatal visits are the backbone of your care. At them, a midwife or doctor checks on you and your baby, usually looking at things like your blood pressure, your wee, your bump size, and later, your baby's heartbeat and position. These visits are where worries get answered and problems get caught early, so keep every one, even when you feel completely fine. Feeling well and still going is exactly how it is meant to work.

The common tests, in plain terms

You do not need to memorise these. You just need to know they exist, so that when your clinic mentions one, you are not frightened by the word.

  • Blood tests. Early on, your blood is usually checked for things like your blood group, whether you are low on iron (anaemia), and immunity to certain infections. It is routine, and it helps your team look after you well.
  • Urine (wee) tests. At many visits, a quick check of your wee looks for signs of infection or other things worth watching. Simple and painless.
  • Blood pressure. Checked at your visits because it is an important part of keeping you and the baby safe. This is one reason the check-ups matter so much.
  • Blood sugar tests. Sometimes a test is done later in pregnancy to check how your body is handling sugar. If it is offered to you, it is a normal part of care, not a sign that something is wrong.

The common scans, in plain terms

  • The early scan (often called the dating scan). Usually in the first trimester, this scan helps confirm how many weeks pregnant you are and gives you an estimated due date. For many mums it is the first time they see their baby on the screen.
  • The mid-pregnancy scan (often called the anomaly scan). Usually around the middle of pregnancy, this is a more detailed scan that looks closely at how your baby is growing and developing. Your clinic will explain what they are looking at.
  • Later scans. Sometimes extra scans are done later, for example to check the baby's growth or position. If you are offered one, ask what it is for, and your team will tell you.

We tell you what these scans are so you can walk in calm and ask good questions. What the scan shows is always for your doctor or the person doing the scan to explain, never for this guide or the internet to interpret.

Pillar 5 · Get Ready for Birth and the First Days

As your due date comes closer, the birth can feel like a wall you are heading towards with your eyes closed. This part turns that wall into a door. When you know the signs of labour, what to pack, what a birth plan is, and what your baby actually needs, you meet the end of pregnancy prepared instead of panicked.

A gentle truth first. Birth rarely goes exactly to any plan, and that is okay. The point of preparing is not to control it. It is to feel steady and informed whatever happens, and to know that your care team is there to guide you through. We never promise you a particular kind of birth, because no one can.

Signs of labour, and when to go

Knowing the signs takes away a lot of the "is this it?" panic. Common signs that labour may be starting or coming close include:

  • Tightenings (contractions) that become regular, get stronger, last longer, and come closer together over time. This is different from the mild, irregular practice tightenings of late pregnancy.
  • A "show," which is a plug of mucus, sometimes tinged pink, coming away.
  • Your waters breaking, as a gush or a slow trickle of fluid.

Your maternity unit will usually tell you at your appointments when to come in, often based on how close together and how strong your contractions are. Follow their guidance first. And keep your safety rules front and centre right to the end: any bleeding, or any change in your baby's movements, means you contact your maternity unit straight away, even in these final weeks, even if you think labour might be starting. If your waters break, tell your maternity unit, especially if the fluid is green, brown, or bloody, or if you are not full term. When in doubt, call them. That is what they are there for.

Your hospital bag

Packing your bag a few weeks before your due date means you are not scrambling when the day comes. You do not need a mountain of things. A calm, sensible bag covers you and the baby for the birth and the first day or two.

Broadly, you will want a few comfortable things for you, the documents your clinic asks for, toiletries and pads, a going-home outfit for you and the baby, some baby basics like bodysuits, nappies, and a blanket, and phone and charger. Your own maternity unit may have its own list, so ask them and follow it.

Rather than write it all out here to forget, build your bag with the hospital bag checklist. Tick things off as you pack, so you can relax knowing it is ready by the door.

Your birth plan

A birth plan is simply a short note of your preferences for the birth: things like who you want with you, how you feel about pain relief, and any wishes for straight after the baby is born. It is not a contract, and it is not a script the birth must follow. It is a way to think things through in advance and to share your wishes with your care team.

Hold it loosely. Birth is unpredictable, and your team may need to change course to keep you and the baby safe, which is exactly their job. A good birth plan says what matters to you and trusts your professionals to guide you on the day. Put yours together gently with the birth plan builder, then talk it through with your midwife or doctor so you are all on the same page.

What the baby actually needs

First-time mums are sold a lot. Shops and adverts want you to buy everything, and it is easy to spend a fortune on things a newborn never uses. The truth is that a new baby needs surprisingly little at the start: somewhere safe to sleep, nappies, a few bodysuits and sleepsuits, blankets, something to feed with, and clothes for the weather. Much of the rest can wait until you know what you and your baby actually use.

You do not have to work out the difference between the essentials and the waste on your own. The baby essentials checklist separates what a newborn really needs from what the adverts push, so you spend on the right things and save on the rest.

The very first days

When the baby arrives, everything is new at once, and that is normal. Newborns feed very often, sleep in short bursts, and cry, sometimes a lot, because crying is how they tell you everything. In the first days you are learning them, and they are learning you. Feeding, whichever way you do it, often takes time to settle, and asking a midwife for help early makes it far easier.

This guide walks you up to the birth and the first days. The moment the baby comes, a whole new stage begins: your own recovery, and life with a newborn. That is exactly where the next part of the journey picks you up, warmly, so you are never left to work it out alone. More on that in your next steps.

And keep your safety instinct switched on for the baby too. If your newborn seems unwell, is not feeding or wetting nappies, is very hard to wake, or your gut says something is wrong, always contact a doctor. Trusting that feeling is good mothering, not fussing.

Bonus · Your pregnancy warning-signs card

Keep this page. Screenshot it or print it. It is your safety net, and it stays yours even if you decide this program is not for you. It does not replace your own gut feeling, so if something feels seriously wrong, get help even if it is not on this list.

  • You have any bleeding from your vagina, at any stage, any amount. Every time. Never wait.
  • Your baby's movements change once you feel them: fewer, slower, or a different pattern than normal. Same-day call, every time. Never wait.
  • You have a bad headache that will not go, especially with blurred sight, flashing spots, or sudden swelling in your face, hands, or feet.
  • You have strong or constant tummy pain, or pain high up under your ribs.
  • You have a high temperature, or feel hot, shivery, and unwell.
  • You are being sick so much you cannot keep food or water down.
  • Your waters break or fluid leaks, especially if it is green, brown, or bloody, or before you are full term.
  • You have burning, pain, or a strong smell when you wee.
  • Anything at all feels seriously wrong to you. Your instinct counts.

For your mind, reach out if:

  • You feel low, empty, or very worried most of the day for more than two weeks.
  • You cannot sleep even when you have the chance, or your mind races with dread.
  • You feel cut off from everyone, or that you are failing before the baby is even here.
  • You ever have a thought of harming yourself. This is an emergency. Get help immediately.

In Nigeria: Mentally Aware Nigeria Initiative (MANI), 0809 111 6264. For a physical emergency, go to your nearest hospital or contact your maternity unit or doctor. Wherever you are in the world, your midwife, doctor, or emergency service comes first. We would always rather you go and be checked than wait.

Quick answers to questions mums ask

I am only a few weeks pregnant. Is it too early to start this?

No. The plan starts from your positive test and walks with you to delivery day. Starting early is when it helps most, because you learn what is coming before it arrives, instead of scrambling to catch up.

Is it normal to feel this sick and tired?

For many women, yes, especially in the first trimester. Small frequent meals, rest, and sipping water or ginger help. If you cannot keep any food or water down at all, that is a call-now sign, so contact your doctor.

Is it normal to feel almost no symptoms?

Yes. Some women sail through the early weeks with very little. It is not a bad sign on its own. Keep every antenatal appointment, and remember that any bleeding, whatever else you feel, always means a call.

What foods do I really need to avoid?

Cook meat, chicken, fish, and eggs properly, wash fruit and vegetables well, go easy on very large fish and on liver, and be careful with unpasteurised milk and soft mould-ripened cheeses. Your Nigerian favourites are fine when cooked through and served hot. Check anything specific with your midwife.

Are herbal mixtures and agbo safe in pregnancy?

Be very careful. Many are untested, the strength is unknown, and some can harm you or the baby. Do not take any herbal mixture, agbo, or traditional remedy without asking your doctor or midwife first.

Can I still exercise?

Usually yes, gently, unless your doctor has said otherwise. Walking, swimming, and pregnancy-safe classes are good. Avoid anything with a real risk of falling, and stop and check with your doctor if you get pain, dizziness, or bleeding.

My baby's movements feel different today. Should I wait and see?

No. Once you feel your baby move, any change in the pattern, fewer or slower or different, means you contact your maternity unit the same day, every time. Never wait, and never feel you are being dramatic. This is exactly the right thing to do.

I saw a little blood. Is that normal in pregnancy?

Treat any bleeding as a reason to contact your maternity unit or doctor straight away, at any stage. Do not wait to see if it stops. Getting checked is always right, and often all is well, but that is for them to confirm, not for you to guess.

What is a birth plan, and do I have to follow it exactly?

It is a short note of your birth preferences to share with your care team. It is not a contract. Birth is unpredictable, and your team may need to change course to keep you and the baby safe. A good plan says what matters to you and trusts your professionals on the day.

How much do I really need to buy for the baby?

Far less than the shops suggest. A safe place to sleep, nappies, a few bodysuits and sleepsuits, blankets, something to feed with, and weather-appropriate clothes cover the start. The baby essentials checklist separates the real needs from the adverts.

Everyone gives me different advice. Who do I listen to?

Trust your own midwife and doctor for medical questions, and trust your own instinct for your body and baby. You can thank people for their advice and still do things your way.
