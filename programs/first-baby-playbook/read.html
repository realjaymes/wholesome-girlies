Welcome

Nobody hands you a manual with the baby.

They hand you a whole new person and send you home. Then the questions start, and they do not stop. Is she feeding enough? Why won't he sleep? Is this rash normal, or is it something? Should he be crawling by now? You reach for your phone at 2am and get ten answers, half of them arguing with the other half.

Then there is everyone else. Your mother did it one way. Your mother-in-law did it another. The aunties have opinions, the WhatsApp groups have opinions, and the woman at the market has an opinion too. Almost none of it was asked for. Some of it is kind. A lot of it is out of date. And all of it lands on you, because you are the one who has to decide.

This is one calm playbook for the whole early stretch, instead of a pile of advice that all says something different. It helps you understand what is normal, get through the sleepless weeks, feed with a little more confidence, read the milestones without panic, and know exactly when something belongs with a doctor. What it will not do is promise you a baby who sleeps through the night, or a milestone hit on a schedule. Real guidance, never false promises.

You are learning a brand-new job with no handover, on very little sleep, with a crowd of voices telling you what to do. That is a lot to carry. You do not have to carry it alone.

How to use this

You do not have to start at the beginning. Open the part you need today. If sleep is what is breaking you this week, start there. If it is the constant "is this normal" worry, start with the first part. The rest will wait.

Take one small thing at a time. You are tired. Read a little, try one thing, come back later. Nothing here runs out.

Start at your baby's stage. This covers the newborn weeks through the toddler years, so jump to the age you are in right now. You do not have to read it in order, and it is never too late to start.

Keep your child's doctor close. This helps you understand what is normal and ask better questions. It does not replace your paediatrician, your nurse, or their check-ups. Anywhere something needs real care, we tell you plainly. When your gut says something is wrong, trust it and call, even if it is not written here.

Your safety first

Before anything else, here are the signs that mean stop and get help. You do not need to memorise them. Just know this page is here, and come back to it whenever your gut is uneasy.

Get medical help now, or go to a hospital, if your baby has:

  • Trouble breathing: fast breathing, grunting, the skin pulling in around the ribs, or a blue tinge to the lips or face.
  • A high fever, especially in a baby under three months old. A fever in a very young baby is always a reason to be seen quickly.
  • A fever with a stiff neck, a rash that does not fade when you press a glass against it, or a baby who is floppy, very hard to wake, or will not respond.
  • A fit or seizure.
  • Choking that does not clear, or a baby who cannot cry, cough, or breathe.
  • Repeated vomiting, a swollen tummy, or signs of dehydration: a sunken soft spot on the head, no wet nappies for many hours, no tears when crying, a dry mouth.
  • A baby who is much more sleepy than usual, will not feed, or seems to be getting worse fast.

Trust yourself. You know your baby better than anyone. If something feels seriously wrong, get help even if it is not on this list, and even if you cannot explain why. Doctors would far rather see a well baby than miss a sick one.

For your own mind: raising a baby is heavy, and low or anxious feelings are common. If you feel low, worried, or not yourself for more than two weeks, or sooner if it feels heavy, please talk to someone trained to help. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, treat it as an emergency and get help straight away. In Nigeria you can call the Mentally Aware Nigeria Initiative on 0809 111 6264. Looking after yourself is part of looking after your baby.

The fuller "when to see a doctor" guidance sits inside Part 5. Keep this page close. Your safety, and your baby's, never depends on you finishing this playbook.

Pillar 1 · Is This Normal?

This is the question behind almost everything in the early years. Is this poo the right colour? Is this rash something? Is she crying too much? Should he weigh more by now? Most of the time, the answer is that it is normal, and knowing the normal range is what turns the panic down. This part is your reassurance core. It helps you tell what is common and fine from what is worth a call.

One rule sits over all of it. This part helps you understand the usual ranges. It does not diagnose your baby, and it never replaces your doctor's eyes on your child. When something falls outside these ranges, or your gut is uneasy, that is your cue to ask a professional.

Nappies, and what they are telling you

You will look at more nappies than you ever imagined, and they are one of the clearest signs that your baby is well.

  • Wet nappies. A rough guide that your baby is getting enough milk is plenty of wet nappies, usually around six or more heavy wet ones a day once feeding is established. Fewer wet nappies, very dark wee, or a baby who seems dry is worth a check, especially with other signs of illness.
  • Poo, in the early weeks. The first poos are a dark, sticky, tar-like colour. That is normal and it passes in the first days. After that, the colour and texture depend on how your baby feeds. Breastfed babies often have soft, runny, mustard-yellow poo, sometimes seedy-looking. Formula-fed babies tend to have firmer, paler poo. Both are normal.
  • How often. Some babies poo after every feed. Others, especially older breastfed babies, can go several days without one and still be perfectly fine, as long as the poo is soft when it comes and the baby is comfortable. Range is the word to hold on to.
  • When to ask a doctor. Get advice if you see very pale, chalky-white poo, or poo that is red with blood, or black poo after the first few days. Hard, dry pellets with a baby straining and in pain can point to constipation, which we cover in Part 5. Watery poo many times over, especially with vomiting, can mean an infection and a risk of dehydration, so get help.

Crying, and what it means

All babies cry. It is how they tell you everything, from "I am hungry" to "I am tired" to "I just need to be held." In the early months, a lot of crying is normal and does not mean you are doing anything wrong.

  • The usual causes. Hunger, a dirty nappy, being too hot or too cold, tiredness, wind, or simply wanting closeness. Working through that short list settles most crying.
  • The evening fussiness. Many babies have a stretch, often in the late afternoon or evening, when they are harder to settle for no clear reason. This is common and usually eases as they grow.
  • The long, hard crying (sometimes called colic). Some babies have spells of intense crying that are hard to soothe, often at the same time each day, in the first few months. It is exhausting and it can make you feel helpless. It is common, it is not your fault, and it usually passes. Soothing ideas are in Part 5.
  • When crying is worth a call. A cry that sounds different from usual, very high-pitched, weak, or a baby who cries and cannot be comforted at all, especially with a fever, being floppy, refusing feeds, or vomiting, needs a doctor. So does your own strong sense that this cry is not right. You know your baby.

Skin, rashes, and the usual marks

Baby skin does a lot of strange things in the early weeks, and most of it is harmless.

  • Common and usually fine: little white spots on the nose (milk spots), a blotchy newborn rash that comes and goes, dry or peeling skin in the first weeks, heat rash in hot weather, and nappy rash from wetness. Cradle cap, which is greasy, yellowish, scaly patches on the scalp, is also common and harmless.
  • Worth soothing at home: nappy rash usually settles with frequent changes, gentle cleaning, air time, and a barrier cream. Heat rash eases when you cool and loosen the baby.
  • Get help quickly for a rash with a fever, a rash that does not fade when you press a clear glass against it, spreading redness that is hot and sore, or blisters and weeping sores. These need a doctor.

Weight and growth, without the worry

Your baby's weight is one of the best signs of health, which is exactly why it can cause so much anxiety. Here is the calm version.

  • Newborns lose a little weight in the first days, then usually get back to their birth weight in the first couple of weeks. This is normal and expected.
  • After that, babies grow along their own line. Health workers plot weight on a growth chart. The important thing is not hitting one exact number, it is that your baby keeps growing steadily along their own curve over time. A chart is a guide, not a race, and not a report card on you.
  • Every baby is their own size. A smaller baby feeding well and meeting their stages is thriving. Comparing your baby to a bigger cousin is a fast route to worry you do not need.
  • When to ask. If your baby is not gaining weight, is losing weight after the early days, or has dropped across the chart's lines, raise it with your health worker or doctor. That is what the weigh-ins are for.

The "common versus call a doctor" habit

The most useful thing this whole pillar teaches is a habit, not a fact. For anything new, ask yourself two quiet questions. First, is my baby otherwise well: feeding, weeing, alert at times, and settling? Second, does my gut say something is off? A baby who is feeding, has wet nappies, and seems themselves is usually fine to watch at home. A baby who is off their feeds, floppy, feverish, breathing hard, or simply not right is a baby to get seen. When in doubt, call. That is never an overreaction.

Pillar 2 · Sleep

If you are reading this on almost no sleep, start here. Sleep, or the lack of it, is the thing that wears first-time mums down more than anything else. This part is about your relief. It helps you understand why babies sleep the way they do, build a gentle rhythm, and get through the hard stretch with less panic.

A promise, and its limit. We will help you understand baby sleep and give you calm, gentle approaches to try. We will never promise that your baby will sleep through the night, because no one can, and every baby is different. What we can do is help you feel steadier and less alone in the sleepless weeks, and that is what actually helps.

One safety rule sits above everything in this pillar, and it never bends.

Safe sleep comes first, always

Before any routine, any technique, any tip, safe sleep is the ground it all stands on. This follows official safe-sleep guidance, and nothing in this playbook ever contradicts it.

  • Back to sleep. Always place your baby on their back to sleep, for naps and at night, unless a doctor has told you otherwise for a medical reason. This is the single most important thing.
  • A clear, flat sleep space. A firm, flat surface with a well-fitting sheet, and nothing loose in the sleep space: no pillows, no soft toys, no thick or loose bedding, no cot bumpers. A clear space is a safe space.
  • Keep the space smoke-free and be careful not to let your baby get too hot. A comfortable room temperature and light layers are safer than heavy wrapping.
  • Think carefully about where baby sleeps. Official guidance is that the safest place for a young baby to sleep is on their own safe surface, in the same room as you, for the early months. There are known risks to sharing a sleep surface, especially a sofa or armchair, or if anyone has been drinking, smoking, or is very tired. If you want to understand the safest arrangement for your family, ask your health worker. This is one to get right rather than guess.

Everything else in this pillar sits inside these rules. If any tip you read anywhere, from anyone, contradicts safe sleep, safe sleep wins.

Why newborns do not sleep like we wish they did

It helps to know the reason, because it stops you feeling like you are failing. Newborns have tiny tummies and need to feed often, day and night. They have not yet learned the difference between day and night, so their sleep is scattered across the whole day in short stretches. This is normal newborn biology, not a habit you have let them fall into, and not a sign you are doing it wrong.

  • Newborn sleep is broken and frequent, and that is expected. Long unbroken nights are not something a very young baby can usually do.
  • Day and night slowly sort themselves out over the early months as your baby matures, and you can gently help this along, which we come to below.
  • Sleep changes in waves. Just when you think you have found a rhythm, it shifts, often around growth spurts or new stages. That is normal, not you undoing your own progress.

Gently building a rhythm

You cannot force a young baby onto a strict timetable, and trying usually just adds stress. What you can do is build a gentle, flexible rhythm that helps your baby learn the shape of the day. Think rhythm, not rigid schedule.

  • Make days bright and busy, nights calm and boring. In the day, keep things light and normal: daylight, gentle noise, play. At night, keep feeds and changes quiet, dim, and low-key, with little talking. This teaches the difference between day and night over time.
  • Watch for tired signs, and act early. Yawning, staring, rubbing eyes, going quiet, or getting fussy often mean a baby is ready to sleep. Settling them at the first signs, before they are overtired, usually makes it easier. An overtired baby is often harder to settle, not easier.
  • A short, simple wind-down helps. Even for a small baby, the same few calm steps before sleep, a feed, a clean nappy, dim light, a cuddle or a soft song, become a signal that sleep is coming. Keep it short and repeatable.
  • Naps matter too. Good daytime sleep often supports better night sleep, because an overtired baby settles worse. The right amount of daytime sleep changes as your baby grows.

Age changes everything about sleep, so treat any "schedule" as a rough guide that you adjust to your own baby. As babies grow, they gradually take fewer, longer naps and more of their sleep at night. You can shape a gentle daily rhythm and nap pattern that fits your baby's age with the Baby Routine Planner tool, and follow how feeds and sleep actually fall across your days with the Feeding and Sleep Tracker tool. Seeing the real pattern often calms the worry all by itself, and helps you spot where a small change might help.

Gentle ways through the hard nights

There is no magic switch, and anyone who promises one is selling something. But there are calm, gentle things that help many families, always inside the safe-sleep rules above.

  • A calm, consistent wind-down, repeated each night, so sleep becomes something the baby's body starts to expect.
  • Soothing that suits your baby: gentle rocking, soft patting, a quiet shushing sound, a comfortable feed, close contact. Babies are soothed by calm, and by you.
  • Sharing the nights where you can. If you have a partner or a helper, taking turns so each of you gets a longer stretch of sleep protects you both. If you breastfeed, someone else can still change and settle the baby so you do less.
  • Being kind to yourself about it. Sleep in the early months is survival, not a project you are graded on. Some nights you will just get through, and that is enough. It does not last forever, even though it truly feels like it will.

If broken sleep is dragging your mood down and it is not lifting, that matters. Please read the note on your own mind in Part 5, and reach out. Your rest and your wellbeing are part of your baby's care, not a luxury.

Pillar 3 · Feeding and Solids

Feeding brings some of the loudest, most confident, most contradictory advice you will ever receive. Breast or bottle, how much, how often, when to start solids, what to start with, what to avoid. This part cuts through the noise and gives you a calm place to stand. However you feed your baby, a fed baby and a coping mum are what matter.

As with everything here, this is guidance, not a prescription for your particular baby. Your health worker and doctor know your child, and anything about your baby's specific feeding or growth goes to them.

Milk in the early months

For roughly the first six months, milk, breast or formula, is your baby's main food, and it stays important well beyond that.

  • Breastfeeding. If you breastfeed, feeding often, on demand, is normal, especially in the early weeks. Feeding very often in bursts, called cluster feeding, is the baby building your supply, not a sign you are running low. A rough sign that feeding is going well is a baby who is gaining weight and having plenty of wet nappies. If feeding hurts a lot, or you are worried about supply, ask a breastfeeding helper or nurse early rather than struggling alone.
  • Formula feeding. Formula is a safe and loving way to feed your baby. Make it up exactly as the tin instructs, with clean preparation, correct measuring, and safe water, so every feed is made and stored properly. Never water it down to stretch it, and never make it stronger. If you are unsure how much or how often, the tin gives a guide by age and weight, and your health worker can help you fine-tune it.
  • Mixing breast and bottle works for many families, and it lets other people share feeds, which can protect your sleep.
  • How much, roughly. Babies are good at showing you when they are hungry and when they are full. Feeding to your baby's hunger and fullness cues, rather than forcing a fixed amount, is usually the calmest path. Steady weight gain and enough wet nappies are your reassurance that it is working.

Nobody should shame you for how you feed. It is your body, your baby, and your choice. If feeding of any kind is making you miserable or unwell, that is a reason to get help and look at your options, not to suffer quietly.

Starting solids

At some point, milk alone is no longer enough, and your baby is ready to start tasting real food. This is an exciting stage, and also one that comes with a pile of conflicting advice. Here is the calm version.

  • When to start. Current guidance is to start solids at around six months, when your baby shows they are ready. Signs of readiness include being able to sit up well with support and hold their head steady, showing real interest in your food, and being able to bring food to their mouth. Ask your health worker about the right timing for your baby, especially if your baby was born early.
  • How to begin. Start slowly, with small tastes once a day alongside their usual milk, and build up gently. Milk is still the main event at first. Soft, smooth, or well-mashed foods are the usual starting point, moving to lumpier textures and soft finger foods as your baby learns.
  • First foods, the Nigerian way. You do not need special or expensive baby foods. Everyday foods work beautifully: soft, well-cooked and mashed pap or ogi, mashed sweet potato or yam, mashed ripe banana or pawpaw, mashed avocado, well-cooked and blended beans, moi moi, soft-cooked vegetables, mashed rice, and blended fruits. Introduce foods one at a time so you can spot anything that does not agree with your baby.
  • A safety note on choking. Always stay with your baby while they eat. Avoid hard, small, round, or firm foods that can block a small airway, such as whole nuts, whole grapes, hard chunks, and anything small and firm. Cut and prepare foods to be soft and manageable for their age. Knowing basic infant first aid for choking, which we cover in Part 5, matters here.
  • What to hold back. Do not add salt or sugar to your baby's food, and avoid honey in the first year. Your health worker can guide you on other foods to introduce carefully, especially common allergy foods, and on any family history worth mentioning.

The fussy-eating stage

As babies become toddlers, many go through a stretch of refusing food they loved last week, eating almost nothing one day and plenty the next, or wanting the same three things forever. This is one of the most worrying stages, and one of the most normal.

  • It is common, and usually passes. A toddler's growth slows compared with the first year, so their appetite naturally drops and swings. A child who is active, growing, and well is very often eating enough over a week, even when a single day looks alarming.
  • Your job and their job. A calm way to think about it: you decide what healthy food is offered and when, your child decides how much of it they eat. Pushing, forcing, or begging usually backfires and makes mealtimes a battle.
  • Gentle things that help. Keep offering a variety without pressure, even foods that were refused before, because it can take many tries before a child accepts something new. Eat together where you can. Keep mealtimes calm and short. Try not to turn food into a reward or a punishment.
  • When to ask. If your child is losing weight, seems unwell, is very limited in what they will eat over a long time, or you are genuinely worried about their growth or nutrition, raise it with your health worker or doctor. That is what they are there for, and it beats carrying the worry alone.

Pillar 4 · Milestones, Growth and Behaviour

Smiling, rolling, sitting, crawling, walking, talking. The milestones are some of the most joyful parts of the first years, and also some of the most anxious, because it is so easy to compare your baby to every other baby and start to panic. This part helps you understand what to expect and roughly when, so you can enjoy the stages instead of fearing them.

The most important thing to hold on to runs through this whole pillar. Milestones happen across a wide range of ages, not on a fixed date. Your baby is on their own timeline. This part helps you know the range and know what is worth watching. It never diagnoses a delay, and it never replaces your doctor. Where you are worried, the answer is always to see your paediatrician, not to judge it yourself from a chart.

What tends to come when, roughly

Here is a gentle map of the usual order, with rough age ranges. Treat every one of these as "around this time, give or take," never as a deadline. Babies reach these at their own pace, and a wide spread is completely normal.

  • The early months: starting to smile socially, following you with their eyes, making cooing sounds, and beginning to hold their head steadier.
  • Around the middle of the first year: rolling over, reaching for and grabbing things, laughing, sitting with support and then without, and babbling.
  • Towards the end of the first year: sitting steadily, moving around by crawling, bottom-shuffling, or their own method (some babies skip crawling entirely, which is normal), pulling up to stand, and saying early sounds like "mama" or "dada."
  • Into the second year: standing, cruising along furniture, first steps and then walking, first clear words, following simple instructions, and pointing at things they want.

Remember two things. First, the order matters more than the exact age, and even the order has exceptions. Second, if your baby was born early, it is fair to judge their stages against their due date, not their birth date, for the first couple of years. Your health worker can explain this for your baby.

You can follow your baby's stages gently, and see the normal range for each, with the Milestone Tracker tool. It is there to reassure and to help you notice, not to turn development into a test.

Growth, explained simply

We touched on growth in Part 1, and it is worth repeating here because it causes so much worry. Your baby is measured and plotted on a growth chart at check-ups. What matters is not landing on one exact number, or matching the biggest baby in the family. What matters is steady growth along your baby's own line over time. A chart is a tool for your health worker to spot a problem early, not a scoreboard, and not a judgement of you as a mother.

When to speak to a paediatrician

Because every baby is different, there is no single "late" that fits all. But there are gentle signposts that are worth a conversation, not a panic. Speak to your health worker or paediatrician if you notice things like these, and remember this is about getting a professional's eyes on it, not about you diagnosing anything.

  • Your baby is not making eye contact, smiling socially, or responding to your voice and to sounds at the stages you would expect.
  • Your baby seems very stiff or very floppy compared with other babies.
  • Your baby loses a skill they had before, such as babbling or a movement they used to do.
  • By the toddler stage, there is no babbling, pointing, or any words, or you are worried about how your child hears, sees, moves, or connects with you.
  • Anything at all about your child's development that worries you, at any age.

Bringing a worry to a doctor early is never an overreaction. If there is nothing to it, you get peace of mind. If there is something to support, early support helps most. Either way, you win by asking. We help you notice and prepare the question. Your paediatrician gives you the answer.

The toddler years: tantrums, teething, and more

As your baby becomes a toddler, a new set of very normal challenges arrives. None of these means you have gone wrong.

  • Teething. Teeth usually start coming through in the second half of the first year, though the timing varies widely. It can bring dribbling, sore gums, chewing on everything, and being unsettled. Something safe to chew and gentle comfort help. A high fever, diarrhoea, or a very unwell baby should not simply be blamed on teething, that is worth a doctor's look.
  • Tantrums. Big feelings in a small body with few words yet. Toddlers get overwhelmed and cannot yet manage or explain it, so it spills out. Staying calm, keeping them safe, naming the feeling, and being a steady presence helps more than reasoning or punishing in the heat of it. They pass, and they are a normal part of growing.
  • Toilet training. There is no single right age, and pushing before your child is ready usually makes it slower and more stressful. Signs of readiness, patience, and a calm, praise-based approach without pressure work best. Accidents are part of it, not a failure.
  • Speech. Talking, reading, singing, and naming things to your child, day in and day out, is the best support for their language, and it costs nothing. Children reach words at a wide range of ages. If you are worried about your child's speech or hearing, have it checked rather than "waiting to see," because early support is easiest when it starts early.

Pillar 5 · When Something's Wrong, and Keeping Baby Safe

This is the part you hope to use least, and the part it is most steadying to have. It does two jobs. First, it helps you recognise the common illnesses of the early years and know when to treat at home, when to see a doctor, and when to go in now. Second, it covers the safety essentials that prevent harm in the first place. Both are about calm, prepared action, not fear.

A clear and firm rule for this whole pillar. Everything here is recognise-and-route. It helps you spot signs and act, and it points you to real care. It is never a substitute for a proper infant first-aid course, and never a substitute for your doctor. For any emergency, get medical help immediately.

Common illnesses, and when to act

Babies and toddlers get ill often, especially once they are mixing with other children. Most of it passes with rest and care at home. The skill is knowing the difference between "watch and comfort" and "get seen."

  • Fever. A fever is the body fighting an infection, and it is common. The key point is the whole child, not just the number. A baby who is feverish but still feeding, weeing, and settling at times is usually one to watch and comfort. But a fever in a baby under three months old always needs a doctor quickly. And any fever with trouble breathing, a rash that does not fade under a pressed glass, a stiff neck, a fit, unusual floppiness or drowsiness, or a baby who is getting worse, is a get-help-now situation. Keep your baby comfortable and offer plenty of fluids, and ask a pharmacist or doctor before giving any medicine to a baby, including the right kind and dose for their age.
  • Coughs and colds. Very common, and usually pass with comfort and fluids. Get help if your baby is breathing fast or with difficulty, the skin is pulling in around the ribs, they are feeding poorly, or a cold seems to be turning into something worse.
  • Vomiting and diarrhoea. Common, and the main risk is dehydration, especially in small babies. Keep offering small, frequent feeds or fluids. Watch for signs of dehydration: fewer wet nappies, a dry mouth, no tears, a sunken soft spot, and unusual sleepiness. Blood in the vomit or poo, a swollen painful tummy, green vomit, or signs of dehydration all need a doctor.
  • Rashes. Covered in Part 1. The one to act on fast is a rash that does not fade when you press a clear glass against it, especially with a fever or an unwell baby. Treat that as an emergency.
  • Constipation. Hard, dry, pellet-like poo with a baby straining and uncomfortable. In a baby on solids, offering water and fruit and vegetables often helps. If it is painful, ongoing, or there is blood, ask a pharmacist or doctor. Remember that a breastfed baby going several days between soft poos is not usually constipated.

The golden thread is the same as Part 1. A baby who is feeding, weeing, and themselves is usually one to watch. A baby who is off, floppy, feverish, struggling to breathe, or getting worse is a baby to get seen. When in doubt, seek help. It is never a waste of anyone's time.

Soothing a crying, unsettled baby

When your baby is crying hard and you have checked hunger, nappy, and comfort, these gentle things soothe many babies, and none of them will ever harm a baby who is otherwise well.

  • Hold them close, skin to skin where you can.
  • Gentle, rhythmic movement: rocking, swaying, a slow walk.
  • A soft, steady shushing sound, or quiet humming.
  • A calm, dim, quiet environment, away from bright light and noise.
  • A feed, if it has been a while.

If your baby is inconsolable and also feverish, floppy, refusing feeds, or the cry sounds wrong to you, that is when soothing stops and getting help starts. And if the crying is wearing you down to the point you feel you cannot cope, it is safe to put your baby down somewhere safe, like their own clear sleep space on their back, step away for a few minutes to breathe, and come back. A short pause is far safer than reaching breaking point. Ask for help. You are allowed to need it.

Keeping your baby safe

Prevention is the quietest, most powerful part of this whole playbook. A few habits stop most common accidents before they happen.

  • Safe sleep, every time. The rules from Pillar 2 are the foundation: on the back, on a firm flat clear surface, nothing loose in the space, not too hot, smoke-free. This is the single biggest safety habit of the early months.
  • Choking and infant first aid. As babies explore with their mouths and start solids, choking is a real risk. Keep small objects and unsafe foods out of reach, cut food safely, and stay with your baby while they eat. Learning what to do if a baby chokes, and basic infant first aid, is one of the most valuable things a parent can do. We strongly encourage you to take a proper hands-on infant first-aid course. This guide can tell you it matters and remind you to learn it. It cannot teach you to do it safely from a page, and it is never a substitute for real training or for emergency medical help. In a choking emergency where the baby cannot cry, cough, or breathe, call for emergency help immediately.
  • Babyproofing the home. Once your baby is on the move, the world becomes a set of hazards you can quietly remove in advance. The big ones: cover or block electrical sockets and trailing cords, put away medicines, cleaning products, and anything poisonous well out of reach and locked where you can, guard against falls from beds, stairs, and windows, keep hot drinks, pots, and the kitchen out of little hands, secure heavy furniture and televisions so they cannot be pulled over, and keep small objects, plastic bags, and cords for blinds away from where your baby can reach. Get down to your baby's level and look at the room from there, and you will see the hazards you miss standing up.
  • Water and heat. Never leave a baby or toddler alone near water, even a small amount, not in the bath, not near buckets or basins, not for a moment. Check bathwater is comfortably warm, never hot. Keep them away from cookers, irons, kettles, and hot surfaces.

Quick answers to questions mums ask

Is it normal for my baby to cry this much?

In the early months, a lot of crying is normal, and evening fussiness is common. Work through hunger, nappy, temperature, tiredness, wind, and closeness. But a cry that sounds different, weak, or very high-pitched, or a baby who cannot be comforted at all, especially with a fever, floppiness, or refusing feeds, needs a doctor. Trust your gut on your own baby's cry.

How do I know my baby is getting enough milk?

Plenty of wet nappies, steady weight gain over time, and a baby who settles after feeds are the reassuring signs. If you are worried about supply or weight, ask a nurse or health worker early rather than carrying the worry alone.

When should I start solids, and with what?

Current guidance is around six months, when your baby shows signs of being ready, like sitting with support and holding their head steady. Start with soft, simple everyday foods like well-mashed pap, sweet potato, banana, or blended beans, one at a time. Ask your health worker about timing for your baby, and see the First Foods Planner for ideas.

My baby is not crawling or walking yet like others. Should I worry?

Milestones happen across a wide range of ages, not on a fixed date, and some babies skip crawling entirely. The order matters more than the exact age. If you are genuinely worried, or your baby has lost a skill, is very stiff or floppy, or is not responding to you, speak to your paediatrician. That is for peace of mind or early support, never a diagnosis from us.

My baby has a fever. What do I do?

Look at the whole baby, not just the number. A baby who is feverish but feeding, weeing, and settling at times is usually one to watch and comfort with fluids. But a fever in a baby under three months, or any fever with trouble breathing, a non-fading rash, a stiff neck, a fit, or unusual drowsiness, needs help now. Always ask a pharmacist or doctor before giving any medicine.

Can I give my baby medicine when they are unwell?

Ask a pharmacist or doctor first, always, because the right medicine, and the right dose, depend on your baby's age and weight, and some are not safe for babies at all. Never guess.

Is it safe for my baby to sleep on their side or tummy?

Always place your baby on their back to sleep, for naps and at night, unless a doctor has told you otherwise for a medical reason. Back sleeping on a firm, flat, clear surface is the safest, and it is the single most important sleep rule.

Everyone gives me different advice. Who do I listen to?

Trust your child's own doctor for medical questions, and trust yourself for your baby. You can thank people warmly for their advice and still do things your way. One calm reference point beats ten loud opinions.

My toddler barely eats and it terrifies me. Is that normal?

Very common. A toddler's growth and appetite slow and swing, so a single alarming day is often fine across a whole week. Offer healthy food without pressure and let them decide how much. If your child is losing weight, seems unwell, or you are truly worried about growth, see your health worker.

Is it too late to start this if my child is already a toddler?

Not at all. This covers the whole early stretch, so start at the stage you are in. The playbook, the tools, and the community stay yours as your child grows.
